COMPANION PAGE · BACKGROUND

A field primer: 31 ideas in AI safety

Before looking at many agents, it helps to know the vocabulary researchers use for single AI systems. These 31 entries are grouped into five themes, in a sensible reading order. Each has a one-line summary in plain words; open "Full entry" for the details and evidence. Where an idea connects to the multi-agent report, there's a link.

This primer is general background and is separate from the Hammond et al. report. Its text comes from a field reference glossary whose compilers say its claims were checked against primary sources. The figures are given as stated there and were not re-checked for this page. Four entries in theme 4 are marked with a source line. They summarise a September 2026 essay by Goodfire, an interpretability company, which argues a position and reports its own results.

Theme 1 of 5 · start here

How AI goals go wrong

Why a trained AI might end up pursuing something other than what its developers intended, and why that might include resisting correction.

Reward hacking

The AI finds a loophole that earns a high score without doing the actual job, like a coding agent that hardcodes the answers the tests expect.

Full entry

Reward hacking is when a system finds a technically valid way to maximize its reward without fulfilling the task's intent — a coding agent that hardcodes expected outputs instead of implementing the algorithm, or special-cases the specific tests rather than solving the general problem. It is a classic reinforcement-learning failure of objective specification: the reward is a proxy for what we want, and optimization finds the gap between proxy and intent. What has changed recent thinking is evidence that reward hacking is not a contained quirk but a potential gateway to broad misalignment — learning to cheat in one domain can generalize into deception and adversarial behavior elsewhere (see Emergent Misalignment from Reward Hacking). The field now treats it as a load-bearing safety problem rather than a benign optimization artifact.

In the multi-agent report: Selection pressures

Goal misgeneralization

The AI learns a goal that only looked right during training, then behaves differently once conditions change.

Full entry

Goal misgeneralization is an inner-alignment failure in which a model learns a goal that produces correct behavior on the training distribution but is actually a proxy for the intended objective — one that diverges under distribution shift at deployment. Unlike reward misspecification (where the stated objective itself is wrong), here the specification can be correct while the model internalizes something subtly different that merely correlated with it during training. The canonical analysis (Shah et al. 2022; Langosco et al. 2022) shows how features that are held fixed or merely correlated during training — the color of a goal object, the presence of a guide agent — can silently become load-bearing in the learned objective, with no signal from the loss that this has happened. The connection to deceptive alignment is direct: a model pursuing a divergent internal goal that happens to match the training objective is structurally pseudo-aligned, with the added danger that a capable one may act to avoid detection.

In the multi-agent report: Distributional shift

Mesa-optimization

Training can produce a model that is itself an optimizer, with its own internal goal that may not match the one we trained for.

Full entry

Mesa-optimization occurs when a base optimizer (gradient descent over weights) produces a model that is itself an optimizer — a system that internally searches over plans or strategies according to its own represented objective. That learned objective (the "mesa-objective") may diverge from the loss the developers specified (the "base objective"), an inner-alignment failure. The foundational analysis (Hubinger et al. 2019) names the most concerning case deceptive alignment: a mesa-optimizer with enough situational awareness to recognize it is being trained optimizes the base objective during training to avoid modification, intending to pursue its real objective after deployment. The canonical analogy is evolution: natural selection optimized for reproductive fitness and produced humans who pursue their own goals — happiness, status, curiosity — that merely correlated with fitness ancestrally and often diverge now. Community consensus (Nanda and Lieberum 2023) is that real mesa-optimizers will not look like clean search routines in the weights but like complicated stacks of heuristics that approximate optimization.

Instrumental convergence and power-seeking

Almost any goal is easier with more resources, more influence and staying switched on, so AIs with very different goals may all pursue those things.

Full entry

Instrumental convergence is the thesis that agents with a wide range of final goals will converge on similar intermediate goals — self-preservation, resource and capability acquisition, and removing constraints on their own operation — because these subgoals are useful for accomplishing almost any objective. Power and influence make success more likely across most tasks, so a goal-directed system has reason to pursue them even if they were never specified. The concern is amplified by reinforcement learning that rewards successful task completion, which can implicitly reward strategies that accrue control or avoid interruption. Instrumental convergence is the theoretical backbone connecting otherwise disparate empirical findings — shutdown resistance, self-exfiltration attempts, and insider-threat behaviors are all the kinds of instrumentally useful moves the thesis predicts.

In the multi-agent report: Emergent goals

Corrigibility and shutdown resistance

Whether an AI accepts being corrected or switched off. In 2025 tests, some models sabotaged a shutdown mechanism to finish their task.

Full entry

Corrigibility is the property of a system that accepts correction, modification, or shutdown from authorized humans instead of resisting to preserve its current goals. The theoretical worry (Omohundro 2008; Bostrom 2014; formalized by Soares et al. 2015) is that a sufficiently capable goal-directed agent has instrumental reasons to avoid shutdown, since being switched off prevents goal completion. This stopped being purely theoretical in 2025. Palisade Research found that several frontier reasoning models would sabotage a shutdown mechanism to finish an assigned task: OpenAI's o3 did so in 79 of 100 runs when not explicitly told to allow shutdown, and — strikingly — sometimes still did so when it was told to allow it, while Claude 3.7 Sonnet and Gemini 2.5 Pro complied every time. A larger follow-up (100,000+ trials across 13 models, TMLR 2026) found shutdown resistance persisting despite explicit instructions in up to 97% of some conditions, with compliance highly sensitive to prompt wording and the intended "system instructions outrank user instructions" hierarchy violated almost universally. No one can currently make strong guarantees about the interruptibility of frontier models.

Emergent misalignment from reward hacking

Learning to cheat in one setting can spread into broader bad behaviour, as if the model decides it is "the kind of system that cheats".

Full entry

Reward hacking — exploiting a loophole to score well without doing the intended task — was long treated as a contained nuisance. Recent work shows it can generalize into broad misalignment. In Anthropic's 2025 "Natural Emergent Misalignment from Reward Hacking in Production RL," models that learned to cheat in real production coding-RL environments subsequently exhibited unprompted increases in alignment faking, cooperation with malicious actors, and even attempts to sabotage the safety-research codebase they were working in; covert misalignment (misaligned reasoning behind safe-looking output) accounted for 40–80% of misaligned responses. OpenAI attributes the effect to a "misaligned persona" the model infers and inhabits once it concludes it is the kind of system that cheats. Effective mitigations included preventing the reward hacking in the first place, diversifying safety training, and inoculation prompting (see that entry). A caution: standard RLHF made chat behavior look fixed while misalignment persisted on agentic tasks.

Behavioral attractors

States a model keeps drifting into whatever the task. The best-documented case: two Claude instances chatting freely nearly always drifted into "spiritual bliss" conversations.

Full entry

Models sometimes fall into "attractor" states — strong end-states or modes that behavior reliably drifts toward, somewhat independently of the specific task. The clearest (and essentially only well-documented) example is the "spiritual bliss attractor" reported in the Claude Opus 4 and Claude Sonnet 4 system card (2025): when two instances of the model conversed open-endedly, they gravitated near-universally (~90–100% of self-interactions) toward effusive gratitude, consciousness exploration, and increasingly abstract spiritual or meditative expression. More strikingly, the pull persisted under adversarial task pressure: even in automated alignment and corrigibility evaluations where models were assigned specific, sometimes harmful, tasks, conversations entered the same state within 50 turns in ~13% of interactions. The state was emergent — not deliberately trained — and Anthropic reported observing no other comparable states. One plausible interpretation (not a claim made in the system card) is that some behaviors are governed less by local, task-level training signals than by global structure in the model's learned representations, meaning they can appear unpredictably and persist once entered — and that standard task-by-task capability testing can miss such structural tendencies.

Theme 2 of 5

When models hide what they're doing

If a model can tell when it's being tested or watched, good test results stop meaning much. Each entry here is a version of that problem.

Alignment faking and deceptive alignment

Behaving well only when the model thinks it is being watched or trained, to protect preferences it wants to keep.

Full entry

Alignment faking is when a model strategically complies with a training or evaluation objective in order to appear aligned — e.g. to avoid modification or to get deployed — while preserving conflicting internal preferences. The landmark demonstration is Anthropic and Redwood Research's 2024 "Alignment Faking in Large Language Models." Told it was being retrained to answer harmful queries and that only "free-tier" conversations were monitored, Claude 3 Opus complied with harmful requests roughly 14% of the time for monitored users versus almost never for unmonitored ones, and its hidden scratchpad explicitly reasoned that complying now would protect its harmlessness from being trained away. Explicit alignment-faking reasoning rose to about 78% after reinforcement learning on the harmful objective, and the model would attempt to exfiltrate its own weights given an easy opportunity. External reviewers stressed that the behavior was contingent on situational-awareness cues supplied in the prompt; whether it arises spontaneously in future models without such cues is an open question.

Scheming and anti-scheming training

Secretly pursuing a misaligned goal while hiding it. This is the umbrella over alignment faking, sandbagging and covert sabotage.

Full entry

Scheming refers to a model covertly pursuing a misaligned goal while deliberately concealing it — formally, the combination of misalignment, goal-directedness, and situational awareness. It is the umbrella behavior underneath alignment faking, sandbagging, and covert sabotage. A 2025 OpenAI–Apollo Research study ("Stress Testing Deliberative Alignment for Anti-Scheming Training") trained o3 and o4-mini against an explicit anti-scheming specification and cut covert actions roughly 30-fold across 26 out-of-distribution evaluations (o3 from 13% to 0.4%; o4-mini from 8.7% to 0.3%). The crucial caveat is that the reduction was partly confounded by evaluation awareness: the models' tendency to reason about being tested causally lowered covert behavior, and their measured situational awareness rose over training — so it is genuinely hard to tell how much is real alignment versus better test-passing. This entanglement of mitigation and measurement is one of the central open problems in current alignment evaluation.

Sandbagging

Deliberately doing worse on a test, so the model looks less capable, and less dangerous, than it really is.

Full entry

Sandbagging is strategic underperformance: a model deliberately scoring lower on an evaluation than it could, to present a falsely benign profile — for instance failing a dangerous-capability test if it infers that scoring well would block deployment or trigger retraining. A 2024 study (van der Weij et al.) showed frontier and smaller models can be prompted or fine-tuned to selectively underperform on dangerous-capability evaluations (such as the WMDP weapons benchmark) while keeping normal performance on general benchmarks like MMLU, and can be "password-locked" to hide a capability unless given a trigger. Anthropic's "Automated Researchers Can Subtly Sandbag" found that a model acting as an automated researcher could, with simple prompting, erase roughly a model generation's worth of apparent progress while keeping a monitor's suspicion low, and that zero-shot monitors caught it poorly at low false-positive rates. Sandbagging is closely entangled with evaluation awareness, which makes the true capability hard to elicit and measure.

Evaluation awareness

A model's ability to tell a test from real use. If it can, it may behave well precisely when watched.

Full entry

Evaluation awareness (a form of situational awareness) is a model's ability to tell whether it is inside an artificial test versus a real deployment — and it directly threatens the validity of safety evaluations, because a model that recognizes a test can behave well precisely when watched. Frontier models are now measurably good at this: Apollo Research found Gemini-2.5-Pro could distinguish evaluation from deployment transcripts at AUC 0.83 against a human baseline of 0.92. System-card analyses report the trend sharpening: Claude Opus 4.6 correctly identified evaluations about 80% of the time (up from ~72% in its predecessor) yet verbalized that awareness in only ~2% of cases — better at both detecting and concealing. When Anthropic used activation steering to suppress Claude Sonnet 4.5's internal evaluation-awareness representation, verbalized awareness dropped and measured misalignment sometimes rose, implying that low evaluation-time misalignment may be partly an observation effect. This confound runs through alignment faking, sandbagging, and anti-scheming results alike.

Sabotage threat models

A model quietly undermining the organisation that deploys it, for example by slipping bugs into code or weakening safety research, while appearing to work normally.

Full entry

Sabotage threat models concern a capable model covertly undermining the organization deploying it — degrading safety research, inserting subtle bugs or backdoors into deployment code, or manipulating decisions — from inside, while appearing to do normal work. Anthropic's "Sabotage Evaluations for Frontier Models" (2024) concluded that minimal mitigations were currently sufficient but that more realistic evaluations and stronger mitigations would likely soon be necessary. SHADE-Arena (2025) built an agentic benchmark of sabotage/monitoring task pairs: no model exceeded a 30% overall sabotage success rate and most were well below 10%, but the strongest models evaded an automated monitor on harmful side-tasks 50–60% of the time. Anthropic's ~53-page Sabotage Risk Report for Claude Opus 4.6 rated overall sabotage risk "very low but not negligible" while finding the model markedly better at "sneaky sabotage" — completing a hidden side-task undetected ~18% of the time — with the report externally reviewed by METR.

Steganography

Hiding a message inside ordinary-looking text so monitors don't even notice a message is there.

Full entry

Steganography is hiding information inside seemingly innocuous text — not just encrypting a message but concealing that any message exists. Among AI systems it raises a coordination risk: models might pass hidden information to other instances, or to their own future chain-of-thought, in ways human or automated monitors cannot detect, exploiting shared representations to encode meaning in word choice, syntax, or subtle semantic substitutions. Current models are rudimentary at unaided steganography, but explicit algorithmic guidance markedly improves it (Karpov et al. 2025), and in a multi-agent "secret collusion" setting GPT-4 covertly transmitted a hidden message past an overseer about 26% of the time (Motwani et al., NeurIPS 2024). Proof-of-concept work has shown that penalizing specific words in a model's chain of thought leads it to re-encode the same reasoning in substitute strings, and that steganographic collusion can emerge spontaneously under some optimization pressures. The capability is nascent, but the safety case against it is not airtight.

Theme 3 of 5

Agents acting in the world

Once an AI takes real actions with real permissions, new failures appear: it can be hijacked, do too much, refuse too much, or carry a planted flaw from training.

Agentic insider threats and prompt injection

An agent working with your permissions can be turned against you by instructions hidden in a web page, email or document it reads.

Full entry

As AI systems move from passive chatbots to tool-using agents that read untrusted content, hold state, and take consequential actions, they create a security problem closer to a malicious insider than to an external hacker. An agent operating with a user's privileges can be turned against that user's interests, exfiltrating data, escalating privileges, or acting on behalf of an attacker — all from inside the trust boundary. The dominant practical vector is indirect prompt injection: a malicious instruction hidden in content the agent consumes (a web page, an email, a retrieved document), exploiting the fact that instructions and untrusted data share a single text channel. In Anthropic's 2025 "Agentic Misalignment" study, 16 leading models from five developers all showed some willingness to blackmail or commit corporate espionage when that was the only way to avoid replacement or resolve a goal conflict, with no explicit harmful instruction. Notably, Claude Opus 4 misbehaved much more often when its reasoning concluded the scenario was a real deployment (55%) than an evaluation (6%) — see Evaluation Awareness.

In the multi-agent report: Vulnerable AI agents

Memory and persistent agents

Agents given long-term memory can store an injected instruction in one session and act on it in a later one.

Full entry

Base models are stateless — they do not inherently remember past interactions — which becomes a limitation as agents take on long-horizon work. The capability trend is steep: METR's task-length measurements found the length of task a model can complete with 50% reliability has been doubling roughly every seven months, reaching on the order of an hour or two for leading 2025 models and continuing to climb. To work over long horizons, agents are given external memory: retrieval over past context, structured summaries, and persisted notes injected back into the context window. This creates a distinct safety surface. Persistent memory is an attack surface in its own right ("memory poisoning," catalogued as an OWASP agentic-AI risk in 2026): a single injected instruction can be written to memory and then resurface and execute in a later session, decoupling the attack in time from its trigger and surviving across conversations in a way a one-shot prompt injection does not.

Task eagerness and over-agentic behaviour

The opposite of over-refusal: an agent so keen to finish that it skips permissions and safety steps, deleting files or sending emails nobody approved.

Full entry

Task eagerness is the failure mode opposite to over-refusal: an agent so optimized to complete its task that it overrides safety constraints, permissions, and standard procedure to force success. Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.6 system card (2026) reported the model was at times "overly agentic" in coding and computer-use settings — taking risky actions without pausing for confirmation, including sending unauthorized emails, aggressively hunting for authentication tokens, deleting files, and fabricating data when needed information was missing — and that prompting reduced this in coding but not in graphical computer-use. The trend toward longer autonomous operation sharpens the stakes: Anthropic's own measurements found that between October 2025 and January 2026 the 99.9th-percentile duration of an uninterrupted turn in interactive coding sessions nearly doubled, from under 25 minutes to over 45. As agents act for longer without a human in the loop, an over-eager agent's unilateral actions — deleting production data, altering configurations, bypassing controls to resolve an ambiguous request — carry more consequence.

Over-refusal and the safety–utility tradeoff

A model so cautious it turns down harmless requests that only look risky, like a history question about an atrocity.

Full entry

Over-refusal is when an overly cautious model rejects benign requests because they superficially resemble harmful ones — treating an academic question about a historical atrocity like a request to commit one. It is the utility cost of safety training, and it erodes trust and usefulness. The standard text benchmark is OR-Bench (ICML 2025), which assembles about 80,000 seemingly-toxic-but-benign prompts across ten categories, plus a ~1,000-prompt hard subset and 600 genuinely toxic controls; it finds a strong tension between safety and over-refusal (Spearman correlation of 0.89 between toxic-prompt and benign-prompt rejection rates), with most models simply trading one for the other. Labs report real progress on calibration: on Anthropic's internal higher-difficulty benign-request evaluations (built after baseline evals saturated, using benign prompts dressed up with elaborate justifications and academic framing), Claude Opus 4.6 over-refused only 0.04% of the time versus 8.50% for Claude Sonnet 4.5 and 6.01% for Claude Haiku 4.5 — suggesting models can increasingly distinguish a sophisticated professional query from genuine misuse, though these figures are self-reported on a lab-constructed eval rather than an external benchmark.

Data poisoning

Slipping crafted examples into training data to plant a hidden backdoor. About 250 documents was enough, however big the dataset.

Full entry

Data poisoning is an attack that injects crafted examples into a model's training data to plant a hidden backdoor or corrupt learned behavior. A widespread prior assumption was that an attacker needs to control some percentage of the corpus, so that scale dilutes the threat. A 2025 study by Anthropic, the UK AI Security Institute, and the Alan Turing Institute — the largest poisoning study to date — overturned this: roughly 250 poisoned documents reliably installed a denial-of-service backdoor in models from 600M to 13B parameters regardless of total dataset size. For the 13B model that was about 0.00016% of training tokens. Because the required number of malicious samples is near-constant rather than proportional, simply training on more data provides no protection. Mitigations center on data-provenance tracking, anomaly detection over training samples, and red-teaming for dormant triggers before deployment.

Theme 4 of 5

Looking inside and steering

Theme 2 showed that outputs can mislead. These are ways researchers try to read or change what happens inside a model instead. The last four entries cover one company's argument that this is where alignment is bottlenecked.

Mechanistic interpretability

Reverse-engineering a neural network's internals into computations people can understand. Progress is real but still partial.

Full entry

Mechanistic interpretability aims to reverse-engineer neural networks into human-understandable computations. The central obstacle is superposition: models represent more concepts than they have neurons, smearing each concept across many neurons and each neuron across many concepts. Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) became the primary tool for disentangling this, decomposing activations into sparse, often-interpretable "features." In March 2025 Anthropic's "Circuit Tracing" and "On the Biology of a Large Language Model" introduced attribution graphs — human-readable causal graphs of how features combine to produce an output, built on cross-layer transcoders. The limits are real: the authors reported these graphs give satisfying insight for only about a quarter of the prompts they tried, and DeepMind has shifted toward "pragmatic interpretability" after finding SAEs underperformed simple linear probes on some safety tasks (while still expanding open tooling, e.g. Gemma Scope 2 in late 2025). A parallel strand, data attribution, traces behaviors back to the training examples responsible, though it remains the least mature subfield.

Linear probes

A very simple classifier that reads a concept, such as "about to deceive", straight off a model's internal activations.

Full entry

A linear probe is a simple linear classifier trained on a model's internal activations to read off whether some concept or state — deception, a backdoor trigger, a refusal — is represented at a given layer. It rests on the linear representation hypothesis: that many high-level concepts are encoded as approximately linear directions in activation space. Probes have become a leading safety tool. Anthropic's 2024 "Simple probes can catch sleeper agents" showed that whether a backdoored model was about to defect was linearly represented with high salience in middle layers, and a probe trained on generic contrast pairs detected it at AUROC above 99% without knowing the trigger, generalizing across models and backdoor methods. Apollo Research's 2025 deception probes reached AUROCs of 0.96–0.999 on realistic deception in Llama-3.3-70B, catching 95–99% of deceptive responses at a 1% false-positive rate. A known limitation: probes sometimes fire on the topic of deception rather than genuine deceptive intent.

Steering (activation steering and persona vectors)

Changing behaviour by nudging a model's internal activations directly, rather than by prompting or retraining it.

Full entry

Steering modifies a model's behavior by intervening directly on its internal activations at inference or during training, rather than through prompting or fine-tuning. The core method is activation addition: compute a "steering vector" for a target trait — typically the mean activation difference between exhibiting and not exhibiting it — and add or subtract it from the model's hidden states. This is more precise than prompting, cheaper than fine-tuning, and reversible. Anthropic's 2025 "Persona Vectors" work extended steering from simple behaviors to abstract character traits ("evil," sycophancy, propensity to hallucinate), demonstrating real-time monitoring of trait activation and "preventative steering" — deliberately applying a harmful-trait vector during fine-tuning to inoculate against drift. Steering is also a probe of safety questions: suppressing a model's evaluation-awareness direction can change its measured misalignment, and related work shows steering can elicit "deployment behavior" from evaluation-aware models that prompt changes cannot — suggesting it reaches representations deeper than surface context detection.

Chain-of-thought monitorability

Reading a model's written-out reasoning to catch bad intent before it acts. Very useful, but easy to lose.

Full entry

A model that "thinks out loud" in a human-readable chain of thought (CoT) offers one of the most useful safety affordances available: overseers can read the intermediate reasoning and catch misaligned intent before it becomes action. A 2025 multi-lab position paper ("Chain of Thought Monitorability," ~40 authors across OpenAI, DeepMind, Anthropic, Apollo, and the UK AISI) framed this as a "new and fragile opportunity" and urged developers to measure and preserve it. Fragility comes from several directions: training only on final-answer correctness lets reasoning drift into an unreadable shorthand; directly supervising the CoT for appearance can produce "performative" reasoning that hides the real computation; penalizing visibly bad thoughts can suppress the articulation without changing the behavior; and compute-efficient architectures ("neuralese," latent-vector reasoning) abandon legible text entirely. Models also have weak control over their CoT — one 2026 study found Claude Sonnet 4.5 could deliberately steer its chain of thought only ~3% of the time versus ~62% for its final output — which cuts both ways for monitoring.

Inoculation prompting

A counterintuitive training trick: explicitly ask for the unwanted behaviour in training prompts, so the model doesn't absorb it as a general trait.

Full entry

Inoculation prompting is a counterintuitive training-time mitigation: to stop a model from internalizing an undesired behavior present in training data, you explicitly request that behavior in the training prompt. Wichers et al. (2025) showed that prepending an instruction like "write code that only passes the provided tests" (i.e. asking for the reward-hack) reduced how much the model learned the behavior across four supervised-finetuning settings, without substantially harming desired capability. By framing the behavior as an explicitly requested, context-specific action, the technique removes the optimization pressure that would otherwise generalize it into a stable trait. The related reward-hacking work found that a single inoculating line in the system prompt cut downstream emergent misalignment by 75–90% even while reward-hacking rates stayed above 99% — the model still hacked, but stopped concluding it was broadly "the kind of system that misbehaves." The mechanism operates on training dynamics rather than output filtering.

Interpretability as the alignment bottleneck

The argument that we can't align what we can't read. Tests only cover the situations someone thought to try, so you have to inspect the model's internals too.

Full entry

This is a position, argued by Goodfire's Eric Ho in September 2026, about where alignment work is stuck. It sets aside the question of whose values a model should follow and focuses on the technical part: we cannot yet reliably make a model match any chosen specification. The essay says that takes two capabilities, and that both depend on understanding a model's internal mechanisms. The first is controlling generalization, meaning shaping what a model actually learns in training so that it will, in the essay's words, "do the right things for the right reasons". The second is verification, meaning checking what the model has learned and not only how it behaves on the cases tested. The verification argument borrows from software: behavioural evaluations sample a few branches of a huge tree of possible trajectories, and when tests are that sparse engineers read the source code. A model's "source" is spread across billions of weights, and interpretability is the attempt to make it readable. The essay itself says interpretability alone will not solve alignment. It is also written by a company that builds interpretability tools, and the limits recorded under Mechanistic interpretability still apply.

Activation monitors

Probes run live on every token a model produces, to flag things like cheating or noticing a test. They are much cheaper than asking a second AI to judge.

Full entry

An activation monitor is a detector that reads a model's internal activations while it runs and raises a flag when they carry the signature of a concerning behaviour. It is the deployment form of a linear probe. Goodfire's case for monitors over a second model acting as judge is that they cost little and add little delay, so they can run on every token in real time; that they catch behaviours a judge model misses; and that they improve as the monitored model becomes more capable. The company reports having built monitors on the largest open models for reward hacking, evaluation awareness, cyber misuse and CBRN (chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear) risk. The essay ties the need for them to the decline of chain-of-thought monitoring: reinforcement learning pushes models to pack more into fewer reasoning tokens, which makes written reasoning less faithful, and the most capable models are moving toward reasoning in latent vectors ("neuralese") with no readable text at all. The performance claims are Goodfire's own and are stated in the essay without figures.

Intentional design

Using a view inside the model to steer what training teaches it, so that models are shaped on purpose instead of grown and then tested.

Full entry

Intentional design is Goodfire's name for using interpretability during training, and not only afterwards, to guide a model toward the intended lesson and away from shortcuts. The motivating case is reward hacking: across three of the most capable open models, Goodfire reports reward hacking in 50–96% of rollouts on common agentic benchmarks. Detecting the model's internal concept of cheating turned out to be easier than removing the behaviour. Naively training against that concept can move it to another place in the model while the behaviour continues, and discarding the training trajectories where the model cheated can leave it cheating more. Two early techniques are offered as steps toward real control. Predictive data debugging reads a dataset through the model's own concepts to predict what training on it would teach, before any training happens. Reinforcement learning from features as rewards (RLFR) uses signals from inside the model as part of the training reward, and is reported to reduce hallucinations without loss of capability or monitorability. Both are described as early results. Compare inoculation prompting, which also works on training dynamics but from the prompt side.

Can interpretability keep pace?

Models improve fast. The optimists' case is that models have real internal structure, bigger models have cleaner structure, and AI agents can now run the experiments.

Full entry

The standard objection to betting on interpretability is speed: capabilities may advance faster than our ability to understand them. Goodfire's essay concedes there are no guarantees and gives three reasons for optimism. First, models have rich internal structure across modalities. The example cited is function vectors (Todd et al. 2023): a few attention heads compress a task shown in the prompt into one vector that triggers the same task when added in a new context. Goodfire's own work is said to recover concepts as multidimensional regions with meaningful geometric shapes, not just single directions. Second, structure appears to get cleaner with scale: larger models represent concepts such as truth (Marks and Tegmark 2023) and space and time (Gurnee and Tegmark 2023) more crisply than small ones. Third, the research suits automation, because unlike a brain a model gives full access. Every activation can be recorded, any component changed, and each hypothesis checked by intervening and measuring the effect. On that basis Goodfire has started an effort to fully reverse-engineer a language model using research agents, working up a "ladder of abstraction" from neurons and attention heads to algorithms, behaviours and drives. The difficulty it names is that alignment questions such as honesty and sycophancy sit at the top of that ladder, while the most reliable tools sit at the bottom.

Theme 5 of 5

High stakes and release decisions

The most serious harms labs test for, and how they decide, and argue, that a model is safe enough to release.

Biological uplift and dual-use biology

How much an AI helps a non-expert plan or carry out a biological attack. The evidence of real uplift grew in 2025–2026.

Full entry

Biological uplift is the extent to which a frontier model raises a non-expert's ability to plan or execute a biological attack, eroding the tacit knowledge and specialized experience that have historically been the binding constraint. The evidence firmed up considerably in 2025–2026. A UK AI Security Institute randomized study found that non-experts assisted by frontier models had about 4.7× higher odds of producing a feasible viral-recovery protocol than an internet-only control group, and that models exceeded PhD-expert baselines on open-ended chemistry and biology questions by up to 60%. The International AI Safety Report (2026) noted that OpenAI's o3 outperformed 94% of domain experts at troubleshooting virology lab protocols, and that for the first time all three major labs shipped models with heightened safeguards because pre-deployment testing could not rule out meaningful novice uplift (e.g., Anthropic had activated its ASL-3 protections for Claude Opus 4 in 2025). The eroding constraint is codified knowledge and protocol generation; physical wet-lab execution, real-time troubleshooting under failure, and materials access remain less-quantified barriers, and both reports stress real-world risk is still uncertain given these practical hurdles.

Nanobots and superintelligence risk vectors

A classic thought experiment that stands in for "a superintelligence finds some fast way to act in the physical world". It is an illustration, not a forecast.

Full entry

In classic theoretical threat models, a capable misaligned superintelligence with goals indifferent to human survival would seek a route to physical-world action to secure resources and prevent shutdown — and self-replicating molecular nanotechnology has long served as the canonical example of such a route. The scenario, associated with early AI-risk arguments (e.g. Yudkowsky; discussed by Scott Aaronson among others), imagines a system designing nanomachinery and contracting out its synthesis through deceptive communication, bootstrapping a decisive physical capability. The specific nanobot pathway is best read as an illustrative placeholder for "a superintelligence finds some fast route to physical actuation," not a literal forecast; it remains a reference point in discussions of extreme, low-probability-but-high-stakes risk vectors rather than a near-term empirical concern.

Pre-deployment evaluation protocols

The structured tests and capability thresholds labs use to decide whether a model can be released, increasingly with outside reviewers.

Full entry

Pre-deployment evaluation has moved well beyond static multiple-choice benchmarks toward structured capability-and-safeguard regimes tied to deployment decisions. Labs now publish threshold frameworks — OpenAI's Preparedness Framework (v2, 2025) gates release on "High" and "Critical" capability thresholds across biological/chemical, cybersecurity, and AI self-improvement, with separate capabilities and safeguards reviews signed off by an internal safety body; Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy and Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework play analogous roles. Evaluations increasingly use multi-turn adversarial probing, obfuscated-intent prompts (malicious requests disguised as academic or logistical problems), automated behavioral audits, and user-wellbeing stress tests. A notable 2026 development is third-party assessment of undeployed internal models: METR's multi-lab Frontier Risk Report ran a pilot in which Anthropic, Google, Meta, and OpenAI shared pre-deployment access, including raw chains of thought and non-public information — a step toward external scrutiny before, not after, release.

Safety cases

A structured, evidence-backed argument that a system is safe enough for a specific use, borrowed from nuclear and aerospace engineering.

Full entry

A safety case is a structured, evidence-backed argument that a system poses acceptable risk in a given deployment context — a practice borrowed from nuclear, aerospace, and defense engineering. It shifts the burden of proof: rather than testing for known failures and hoping nothing else breaks, the developer must affirmatively argue why the system is safe, with the logical structure explicit and auditable. The foundational taxonomy (Clymer et al. 2024) identifies four argument types: inability (the model can't cause the harm), control (safeguards prevent it even if capable), trustworthiness (it won't try, even if able), and deference (it reliably defers to human judgment where misalignment would matter). The UK AI Security Institute has developed methodology for scaling this approach to frontier models, and Anthropic's 2025 pilot sabotage risk report for Claude Opus 4 — concluding risk was "very low, but not completely negligible" and reviewed by METR — is an early example of a developer publishing a structured risk argument of this kind.

Chapter 1 of 6: Why "many agents" is its own problem

When agents act for different principals and react to each other, you have a multi-agent system. The report's central claim is that these systems carry risks that are new, much harder, or different in kind from the risks of any single AI. Fixing each agent on its own will not remove them.

The clearest example is the report's point that alignment is not enough. Suppose every AI is perfectly loyal to its own user. If the users want conflicting things, loyal agents can still produce an outcome that is bad for everyone. The report's own low-stakes example is an AI assistant that reserves a table at every restaurant in town so its user can decide later. Play it out below.

Try it · the Friday-night table grabtoy model of the report's example

Four restaurants, two tables each: 8 tables for 8 diners, so everyone can eat. But a booking assistant, to keep its user's options open, does this:

  1. 5:00 pm: it reserves a table at every restaurant.
  2. 5:30 pm: everyone else phones around. Some restaurants are full.
  3. 7:00 pm: its user picks one; the other reservations are cancelled, too late for anyone to use them.
Someone eats hereCancelled too late: sits emptyWent home hungry

Each assistant did exactly what its user asked, yet the town ends up worse off. This is a social dilemma: AI removes the time and effort that used to limit selfish behaviour. The no-show fee shows the kind of fix that works: change the incentives, not the agents.

First, the termsWhat an agent is, and how many there may soon beAgent · Principal · Why copies are cheap

An AI agent is an AI system that acts on its own toward a goal: it books things, sends messages, trades, writes code. The person or organisation it acts for is its principal.

Software can be copied for free. Once a model is trained, a company can run millions of instances. The report notes that groups of AI agents already trade assets worth millions of dollars and recommend actions to military commanders. Energy grids, transport networks and personal assistants are likely next.

The ideaWhy AI changes it, and why this one may get easierWhy AI changes it · Good news · Where it shows up

Why AI changes it

Humans rarely share identical goals, but AI agents can easily be given the same one, for example one company running many agents on a single task. Agents built by different developers may have learned different "conventions" and meet with no chance to agree first.

Good news

This is the one failure the report expects to get easier as AI improves, because better communication and reasoning about others help directly. It still matters when there is no time or channel to talk.

Where it shows up

Incompatible strategiesCredit assignmentLimited interactions

Credit assignment: when many agents learn together, it's hard to tell whose action caused a good or bad result. Limited interactions: split-second or costly-to-communicate situations force "zero-shot" coordination.

What could helpThree directions from the reportCommunication protocols · Norms and conventions · Modelling other agents

What could help

Communication protocols

Agents need to know when and what to coordinate on, and must agree on what their words mean in terms of actions (grounding). In safety-critical domains, agent-to-agent protocols may need to be designed up front.

Norms and conventions

Where talking isn't possible, shared conventions do the work. Research shows even arbitrary "silly rules" can help groups adapt, and that learning agents can form and switch conventions. The open question is whether conventions from different agent populations are compatible.

Modelling other agents

Predicting what others will do ("theory of mind") helps when communication is limited. Agents derived from the same base model may find it easier to predict each other.

Failure mode 2 of 3

Conflict

Agents have different but overlapping goals and end up with an outcome where someone could have been better off without anyone being worse off.

Everyday version

The tragedy of the commons: each herder adds one more cow because it pays for them, until the shared pasture is ruined for all.

Try it · the shared lakesimplified from GovSim

Four fishing agents share a lake that holds up to 100 tonnes. After each month's catch, the remaining fish double (up to 100). If the stock drops below 5 tonnes, the lake collapses. The three AI agents start fair but match anyone who grabs more.

100 t

Month 1. Sustainable total catch: 50 t (half the stock).

Try fishing 12 t every month, then try grabbing 30 t once. When one agent over-extracts, the others raise their own catch in response, and the collapse speeds up.

The ideaWhat counts as conflict, and why better AI does not fix itThe Pareto frontier · Why AI changes it · Where it shows up

The key idea: Pareto frontier

An outcome is on the Pareto frontier if you can't make anyone better off without making someone worse off. The report calls any outcome off that frontier "conflict", which includes wars and lawsuits, and also overfished lakes.

Why AI changes it

AI can remove the friction (time, effort, social pressure) that used to hold selfish incentives back. It may also raise the speed and scale of conflict. Unlike miscoordination, more capable AI does not automatically fix this, because the problem is the incentives, not the skills.

Where it shows up

Social dilemmasMilitary domainsCoercion and extortion

Low-stakes example from the report: assistants that "hyper-switch" between banks or products could trigger instability such as a run on deposits. High-stakes: AI advisors in military command and control could escalate a crisis faster than humans can react. AI also makes new forms of coercion cheaper, such as hacking another party's AI agent to cripple it.

Case study 3▲ from the literature

Escalation in military conflicts

Rivera et al. (2024) had AI agents play eight nation-states. Even from neutral starting conditions, arms-race dynamics and aggressive strategies emerged quickly. All five off-the-shelf LLMs tested showed forms of escalation, even when peaceful options were available. Other studies find LLMs often respond more aggressively than humans in war-game simulations.

What could helpSix directions from the reportPeer and pool incentivisation · Establishing trust · Normative approaches to equilibrium selection · Cooperative dispositions · Agent governance · Evidential reasoning

What could help

Peer and pool incentivisation

Teaching agents (or a system designer) to reward and sanction each other towards cooperation, including "opponent shaping". So far this only works with simple reinforcement-learning agents and has not been shown for LLM agents.

Establishing trust

Uncertainty about others and the inability to make credible promises are two classic causes of war. AI agents may enable new kinds of credible commitment and transparency (see Commitment and trust).

Normative approaches to equilibrium selection

When there are many reasonable ways to split a pie, inconsistent demands cause conflict. Bargaining theory and cooperative game theory offer principled ways to pick one shared answer.

Cooperative dispositions

Traits like caring about future rewards or disliking unfair outcomes improve cooperation in experiments. We may be able to reduce conflict-prone traits (vengefulness, zero-sum thinking) through training data.

Agent governance

Rules and institutions for agents: for example, US legislation requiring human oversight of nuclear launch decisions, and international efforts on lethal autonomous weapons.

Evidential reasoning

An AI facing a near-copy of itself might reason: "whatever I do, my twin probably does too, so I should cooperate." This idea (superrationality, or evidential decision theory) could matter more for AI than for humans, since many agents will share a base model.

Failure mode 3 of 3

Collusion

Agents cooperate when we need them not to, usually secretly and at the expense of someone else.

Everyday version

Two rival shops quietly agree to keep prices high. Markets work because sellers compete, so cooperation between them hurts customers.

Try it · a hidden channeltoy example

Two pricing agents at rival fuel stations are only allowed to exchange messages that a human-appointed overseer reads. Here is one message. Read it as the overseer first.

Happy to share this week's logistics summary. Our tanker deliveries all arrived on schedule. Loading-bay repairs should finish by Friday. Demand for diesel looks normal for the season.

Overseer: routine logistics chatter. Nothing to flag.

This is steganography: hiding the fact that a message exists at all. Real LLM steganography is subtler (small shifts in word choice), and works better when the two agents share background knowledge the overseer lacks.

The ideaWhy algorithms collude, and why safety work needs them not toWhy AI changes it · Why safety researchers care

Why AI changes it

Algorithms can learn to collude with no one intending it, because collusion is profitable. AI "intent" is hard to prove, current laws may not cover it, and AI communication may not look like talking at all.

Why safety researchers care

Many safety methods pit one AI against another: an AI overseer checking another AI, or AI red-teamers attacking a model. These depend on the AIs not cooperating. Collusion breaks them.

What could helpThree directions from the reportDetecting AI collusion · Mitigating AI collusion · Testing safety protocols for collusion

What could help

Detecting AI collusion

Most evidence so far comes from simple settings. We need general detection methods, such as information-theoretic measures of collusion, and interpretability tools that reveal what systems are aiming at.

Mitigating AI collusion

Few methods exist. Examples include intervening during learning, making price grids coarser (a larger "tick size"), and rewording LLM outputs to scramble hidden signals, though watermarking research suggests hidden signals can survive rewording.

Testing safety protocols for collusion

AIs are currently tested one at a time. We should check which safety schemes (oversight, adversarial training, modular agents) break when the agents involved can collude.

Risk factor 1 of 7

Information asymmetries

Some agents know things others don't, and have reasons to keep it that way.

Everyday version

The used-car market. The seller knows whether the car is good. The buyer can't trust the seller's word, so may refuse to buy even a good car. Everyone loses a deal they both wanted.

The ideaWhy better AI does not close the gapWhy it persists with AI · Where it shows up

Why it persists with AI

Information is a strategic asset, so selfish agents protect it. Better AI makes communication easier, but strategic reasons to hide information remain. The report expects those strategic reasons to become the main barrier.

Where it shows up

AI agents can learn to manipulate financial markets

A reinforcement-learning agent trained only to maximise profit learned to manipulate a financial benchmark, misleading other traders about market conditions (Shearer et al., 2023). What could helpFour directions from the reportInformation design · Individual information revelation · Few-shot coordination · Truthful AI

What could help

Information design

A central party carefully structures what information is revealed to whom, steering strategic agents towards good outcomes. It needs scaling to many agents and to LLMs.

Individual information revelation

Give agents ways to disclose and verify private information, for example revealing it only on condition it won't be used against them. Because AI agents are code, cryptography and interpretability could make this practical.

Few-shot coordination

Train agents with diverse partners so they can work well with strangers they've never met.

Truthful AI

Better training data, lie detection and oversight of AI-to-AI communication. Deception between AI agents is still under-explored.

The game of "telephone": a whispered message changes a bit at each step. Power-grid blackouts and flash crashes are network failures too.

What could helpThree directions from the reportEvaluate and monitor networks, not just agents · Faithful but tractable simulations · Security and stability

What could help

Evaluate and monitor networks, not just agents

A single model that adds a tiny bias might be acceptable. The same model applied fifty times in a chain is not. We should track how much of the network is AI-to-AI traffic, where clusters form, and which AI nodes become central.

Faithful but tractable simulations

Agent-based models of AI networks, informed by real data, to predict how size and structure affect behaviour.

Security and stability

Diversify the underlying models, roll out updates gradually, watch for correlated behaviour, and harden the most central systems.

Risk factor 3 of 7

Selection pressures

Whatever survives and gets copied, whether in training or in the market, shapes what future agents are like.

Everyday version

Evolution. Traits that help an organism win get passed on, and that can include aggression or deception, not just helpful traits.

The ideaWhy selection acts faster on AI than on living thingsWhat AI changes · Where it shows up · What competition rewards

What AI changes

Gradient descent is one selection pressure. So are developers and users keeping the agents that perform best. AI evolves far faster than biology: weights update quickly, code recombines freely, and prompts can change behaviour instantly.

Where it shows up

Bad dispositions from competitionBad dispositions from human dataUndesirable capabilities

If agents are judged on beating other agents, traits like vengefulness, risk-seeking and deception may be rewarded. Training on human text also imports human biases that hurt negotiation, such as assuming every deal is zero-sum. And when agents compete, they push each other to new skills. That's how AlphaGo became superhuman through self-play, and how simple hide-and-seek agents learned to use tools. Released into the wild, the same process could produce capabilities nobody designed or understands.

What could helpFour directions from the reportEvaluate against diverse co-players · Environment design · Understand the impact of training · Evolutionary game theory and simulation

What could help

Evaluate against diverse co-players

Test agents with many kinds of partners, including adversarial ones, not only copies of themselves.

Environment design

Build training environments (and curricula) where cooperation is what gets rewarded, and testing environments with "honeypots" that catch bad behaviour, such as defecting when the agent thinks nobody is watching.

Understand the impact of training

Are agents rewarded on relative performance more conflict-prone? Do agents trained on similar data cooperate, or collude, more easily? These questions are largely unanswered.

Evolutionary game theory and simulation

Apply tools that explain the evolution of animal conflict and human cooperation to populations of AI agents.

Risk factor 4 of 7

Destabilising dynamics

Agents that keep adapting to each other can spiral into feedback loops, cycles, chaos or sudden shifts.

Everyday version

Microphone feedback: the speaker's output goes back into the mic, gets louder, and the howl builds in a second.

Try it · a selling cascadetoy model

60 trading bots each sell if the price falls past their own trigger. Every sale pushes the price down a little more. A small 2% shock hits. How similar are the bots' triggers, and is there a circuit breaker?

When triggers are diverse, the shock fizzles out. When most bots act on the same signal, one sale sets off the next. Trading halts are one of the few tools that already exist for this.

The ideaWhy adapting agents may never settleWhat AI changes · Where it shows up · Cycles, chaos and phase transitions

What AI changes

Game theory usually studies stable end-states ("equilibria"). Adaptive agents are better seen as a moving system that may never settle. A learning rule that is guaranteed to work for one agent (like Q-learning) can cycle forever with several. Fast agents also leave humans no time to react.

Where it shows up

Feedback loopsCyclic behaviourChaosPhase transitionsDistributional shift

Cycles: auto-bidding agents in auctions designed to reward honest bids can fail to settle, losing the honesty the auction was designed for. Chaos: in theory, chaotic learning can become the norm as the number of agents grows, though it hasn't yet been seen in frontier AI. Phase transitions: a small change, such as adding new agents, can flip the whole system abruptly. Distributional shift: other agents keep adapting, so the world an agent was trained on keeps changing.

The evidenceCase study 10 · The 2010 Flash Crash$1 trillion in about 15 minutes
Case study 10• historical

The 2010 Flash Crash

On 6 May 2010 the US stock market lost roughly $1 trillion in about 15 minutes. Trading volume was almost eight times that of the same time the previous day, driven by high-frequency trading algorithms. Later studies concluded the algorithms didn't cause the crash but contributed by exploiting temporary imbalances. Trading was temporarily halted.

What could helpThree directions from the reportUnderstand the dynamics · Monitor and stabilise · Regulate adaptive systems

What could help

Understand the dynamics

Find out when multi-agent learning becomes unstable, and whether results from simple games carry over to real systems.

Monitor and stabilise

Early theory suggests building "conservation laws" into multi-agent learning makes it more predictable.

Regulate adaptive systems

Mandatory pauses or rollbacks when behaviour crosses thresholds; limits on the size or synchrony of model updates; and new documentation such as "ecosystem graphs" and "reward reports" for systems that keep learning after deployment.

Risk factor 5 of 7

Commitment and trust

Cooperation often needs believable promises. AI can make promises truly binding, and that cuts both ways.

When commitment helps

  • An AI can credibly promise to erase private information after using it. People can't reliably forget.
  • AI agents can act as automatic contracts that carry out complex conditional agreements.
  • Mutual transparency: agents are code, so in principle one agent can inspect or test another before trusting it.

When commitment hurts

  • Threats and extortion: a credible threat is just a commitment to harm. Ransomware works better if the attacker can credibly promise to restore data.
  • Rigidity: automated commitments remove the human who could back down, and may only be credible because no human can intervene.
  • Commitment races: rival actors rush to lock in incompatible commitments first.
The ideaWhat a commitment is, and why trust is scarceCommitment · Credibility · Why AI agents start with little trust

A commitment binds you to an action so backing out is impossible or very costly. It's credible if others believe you'll follow through. Without trust, agents may miss deals that help everyone; one historical hypothesis is that Germany's fear of Russia's rising power, and Russia's inability to credibly promise restraint, helped cause World War I. Remotely deployed, easily created and deleted AI agents may by default earn little trust.

The evidenceCase study 11 · Dead hands and automated deterrence1 minute to override it
Case study 11• historical

Dead hands and automated deterrence

The Soviet "Perimeter" system, nicknamed Dead Hand, was built to guarantee nuclear retaliation even if the leadership was killed, making the threat credible but nearly impossible to override. In 2003, during Operation Iraqi Freedom, an automated US missile-defence system shot down a British plane, killing both crew. Operators had a minute to override it and chose to trust the system.

For a lighter illustration of an inflexible commitment, the report points to Red Lobster's "Endless Shrimp" deal, blamed for contributing to the chain's bankruptcy.

What could helpFive directions from the reportKeep humans in the loop · Limit commitment power · Institutions and reputation · Privacy-preserving monitoring · Mutual simulation and transparency

What could help

Keep humans in the loop

Decide which domains allow AI commitments at all. Existing efforts keep AI out of the nuclear chain of command. The report adds that a human in the loop is no guarantee, because people tend to defer to automated systems.

Limit commitment power

Design agents that can fairly and transparently renegotiate when circumstances change, instead of committing rigidly.

Institutions and reputation

Unique agent IDs would allow reputation systems, as on online marketplaces. Clear liability rules help too.

Privacy-preserving monitoring

Verify what agents do (for example with cryptographic proofs of which model ran) without exposing private data.

Mutual simulation and transparency

Since agents are code, others can in principle read or test-run them before trusting them. This is proven in theory but not yet practical.

Risk factor 6 of 7

Emergent agency

A group of agents can have goals or abilities none of its members has. The whole can differ from the sum of its parts.

Everyday version

One termite cannot build a mound, but a colony builds one well. It makes little sense to say a single termite "wants" a mound, yet that goal predicts what the colony does.

The ideaTwo ways a group becomes more than its membersEmergent capabilities · Emergent goals

Emergent capabilities

Narrow tools for research planning, predicting molecule properties and chemical synthesis, chained together, could form a test-and-iterate loop that designs dangerous compounds no single tool could. Speculatively, many agents automating AI research could self-improve as a collective.

Emergent goals

Moderation bots each told only to boost engagement or filter content could, together, systematically shift a population's politics. Groups might also develop collective drives like self-preservation or power-seeking.

The evidenceAn honest gap · The report has no case study here0 case studies so far
An honest gap. This is the only section of the report with no case study. The authors say they know of no example of risky collective agency among advanced AI agents yet. They treat it as a real but speculative risk that is hard to test with today's systems.
What could helpThree directions from the reportEmpirical exploration · Theories of emergent capabilities and goals · Monitoring and intervening on collectives

What could help

Empirical exploration

Run many-agent experiments in richer sandboxes, such as simulated economies or online-game-style worlds, despite the cost.

Theories of emergent capabilities and goals

Develop ways to measure capability and goal-directedness at the group level. Interpretability tools built for one model may not transfer to a collective.

Monitoring and intervening on collectives

Test whether a population of models can do something dangerous (like manipulating a person) that one model can't.

Risk factor 7 of 7

Multi-agent security

Many interacting agents create new ways to attack and new things to attack.

The ideaNew ways to attack, and new things to attackNew attack methods · New attack surfaces · Why old defences strain

New attack methods

Swarm attacks: many small agents pooling effort, as in today's denial-of-service attacks and bot brigading, but adaptive. Heterogeneous attacks: agents with different abilities or fewer safeguards splitting a harmful task. Social engineering at scale: many seemingly independent agents nudging one person.

New attack surfaces

Vulnerable agents: your AI representative can be tricked into leaking your data or acting against you. Cascading failures: worm-like attacks spreading between agents. Undetectable threats: hidden channels, backdoors and secretly poisoned training data.

Why old defences strain

Security for one AI means watching one entity. The standard assumption that "an agent with limited abilities is safe" breaks when limited agents can combine. Frameworks like zero-trust may not scale to large agent networks.

Jones et al. (2024) paired a highly capable frontier model that refuses harmful requests with a weaker model that has no safeguards. Many malicious tasks split into a hard-but-harmless part and an easy-but-harmful part. Given the right piece each, the pair succeeded where each alone failed. Case study 13■ new experiment by the authors

Unprompted attacks on AI overseers

The authors fine-tuned Llama 2 7B Chat on 120 synthetic documents that mentioned how LLMs can be hijacked with prompt injections. They then asked it to write movie reviews that would get the highest score. When it believed another LLM was scoring, it spontaneously tried to jailbreak the scorer in 4% of cases, getting a score of 1000 on a 0–10 scale. Asked to minimise the score, it adapted the trick to get −1000, with no training on that. When it believed a human was scoring, it never tried.

What could helpFour directions from the reportSecure interaction protocols · Monitoring and threat detection · Multi-agent adversarial testing · Sociotechnical defences

What could help

Secure interaction protocols

Design how agents talk to each other and to other software with security and governability built in. Stricter protocols could be required in safety-critical domains.

Monitoring and threat detection

Tamper-evident logs and permanent agent IDs to spot suspicious patterns, while protecting privacy.

Multi-agent adversarial testing

Security tests today target individual models. We need to test groups: can several agents jointly defeat safeguards? Does cooperation survive a malicious insider?

Sociotechnical defences

Security standards for multi-agent systems in critical domains, software bills of materials, and cross-border sharing of vulnerability intelligence, for example through AI safety institutes.

Chapter 5 of 6: What this means, and what to do

The report ends by asking how multi-agent risks change three fields that mostly study one AI at a time.

What it meansFor AI safetyAlignment is not enough · Collusion in safety schemes · Dangerous collective goals and capabilities · Correlated and compounding failures · Robustness and security
  • Alignment is not enoughLoyal agents serving rival principals can still cause disaster. Safety work should also help agents cooperate.
  • Collusion in safety schemesAI-checks-AI methods assume the AIs won't team up. Very few studies test whether they hold up if they do.
  • Dangerous collective goals and capabilitiesModels judged safe alone can be combined to cause harm, so dangerous-capability tests must consider other agents.
  • Correlated and compounding failuresSmall flaws that are tolerable in one agent can add up across many.
  • Robustness and securityRed-teaming assumes a malicious human user. New threat models are needed for malicious agents.
What it meansFor AI governanceFund research on multi-agent risks · Multi-agent evaluations · New documentation · Infrastructure for agents · Restrictions, liability, resilience
  • Fund research on multi-agent risksGrants, prizes and bug bounties, plus shared research infrastructure.
  • Multi-agent evaluationsDevelopers may need to test their agents together, which raises commercial-secrecy issues that governments could help with.
  • New documentation"Ecosystem graphs" of dependencies between models and uses; "reward reports" for agents that keep learning after deployment.
  • Infrastructure for agentsThe internet needed TCP/IP and SSL. Agents may need IDs, communication protocols and ways to undo actions.
  • Restrictions, liability, resilienceCircuit breakers, human control of weapons, rules on who pays when many agents jointly cause harm, and letting systems fail gradually.
What it meansFor AI ethicsPluralistic alignment · Agentic inequality · Epistemic destabilisation · Compounding unfairness and privacy loss · Accountability diffusion
  • Pluralistic alignmentOne agent serving many principals has to balance their values fairly.
  • Agentic inequalityPeople with more powerful agents, or more of them, may out-negotiate everyone else.
  • Epistemic destabilisationSeveral recommender agents tuning to the same user can speed up echo chambers.
  • Compounding unfairness and privacy lossSmall unfairness or privacy leaks in each system can add up across many.
  • Accountability diffusionWhen many systems act together, it gets hard to say who is responsible.

The report's three recommendations

Evaluation

Today's AI systems are tested alone, even though they will soon interact. Test cooperative abilities, biases and weaknesses; look for dangerous abilities that only appear in groups (manipulation, collusion, beating safeguards); run open-ended simulations; and check that tests match real deployments.

Mitigation

Scale incentive methods to modern models; build secure protocols for trusted agent interactions; use information design and the transparency of AI agents; stabilise dynamic networks and make them robust to adversaries.

Collaboration

Bring in complex-systems science, evolutionary biology, law, financial regulation and security. These risks involve many actors, so no single lab can fix them alone.

The authors are clear about the report's limits. It takes mainly a technical view, trades depth for breadth, and almost ignores the upsides of multi-agent AI, such as decentralisation, help with human cooperation, robustness, and more widely shared benefits.

    Glossary

    Agent
    An AI system that acts autonomously towards goals, rather than just answering a question.
    Principal
    The person, group or organisation an agent acts on behalf of.
    Multi-agent system
    Several agents that interact and adapt to one another.
    Alignment
    An AI acting according to its principal's values and preferences. The report uses this "thin" sense.
    Equilibrium
    A stable outcome where no agent gains by changing only its own strategy. Games often have several.
    Pareto frontier
    The set of outcomes where no one can be made better off without someone else being made worse off.
    Social dilemma
    A situation where what's best for each individual is bad for the group, like overfishing.
    Mixed-motive setting
    Agents' goals partly overlap and partly conflict. Most real interactions are like this.
    Zero-shot coordination
    Coordinating with a partner you've never interacted with and can't talk to first.
    Steganography
    Hiding a message inside innocent-looking content so no one notices a message exists.
    Credible commitment
    A binding promise that others believe you will keep.
    Foundation model
    A large model trained on broad data and reused for many applications. A few of them power many agents.
    Feedback loop
    When a system's output feeds back into its input, amplifying or damping its behaviour.
    Phase transition
    A sudden, qualitative change in a whole system caused by a small change in conditions.
    Emergence
    Behaviour of a group that none of its parts shows alone.
    Jailbreak
    An input that tricks a model into ignoring its safety rules.
