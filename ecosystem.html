Control and incentives

Who decides, and what do they stand to gain?

The map above shows who is tied to whom but not who holds the right to decide. This section follows five decisions that shape AI safety and asks, for each one, who holds the right, who can block it, and what interests are on record.

It rests on 124 rows of added evidence with a cutoff of 4 October 2026, retrieved on 5 October 2026. It is a verified extension with stated gaps, not a census. High confidence means a primary source supports the claim as stated, including where the claim is only an organisation's own policy. It does not certify that the policy is followed. A blank means not established, never zero. No funding or employment tie is treated as control unless a document grants a right.

  1. What gets researchedA handful of funders decide, by documented internal rules. That is authority over grants, not over the organisations that receive them.
  2. What gets evaluated and publishedEvaluators rely on the labs for model access. Their published policies differ widely, and no signed agreement has been seen.
  3. Whether a model shipsEach lab decides for itself. OpenAI and Anthropic name a body beyond management with a formal role, although no use of those powers has been identified.
  4. What rules bindBinding duties apply in the EU and California now, and in New York from 2027. US federal action is largely voluntary.
  5. Who sits where, and who holds an interestSome funders' principals invested in Anthropic in 2021, and a few people hold roles in more than one place. Recusals and one resignation are also on record.
Decision 1 of 5

What gets researched

Independent safety research runs largely on grants, so the question is who signs them off.

Established

  • Coefficient Giving. A typical grant needs two active approvals: the primary grant investigator and one additional signatory. Exceptions and dollar thresholds are not specified.
  • Survival and Flourishing Fund. The funders, Jaan Tallinn and Dustin Moskovitz, choose how much weight each recommender gets and adjust the final amounts after the recommender process.
  • Manifund. Donors pick regrantors and give them budgets. Regrantors recommend, and Manifund may veto any grant.
  • Other funds. The Frontier Model Forum now manages the AI Safety Fund directly. Longview's Frontier AI Fund is discretionary. Macroscopic and the Transformative AI Fund name the people in charge. None publishes approval thresholds.
  • Largest recorded awards. Coefficient Giving announced USD 160m approved for Resolution, over USD 70m recommended for Redwood Research across two years, and USD 68m recommended for Epoch AI across three. An announced approval is not proof of a binding commitment or a payment.
  • One recipient in detail. MATS publishes its receipts. In 2025 it recorded USD 11.8m and USD 3.6m from Coefficient Giving through two intermediaries, and USD 1.5m and USD 3.0m from Good Ventures.

Not established

  • No organisation's top three funders as a share of its annual budget. This was the central question and it is still open.
  • Expense totals exist for some recipients (METR USD 8.2m and FAR.AI USD 8.6m for 2024, GovAI USD 11.9m for 2025). An expense total is not a budget, and a multi-year award cannot be divided by it.
  • Longview's first disbursement report is offered to prospective donors above USD 100,000 and is not published openly.

What would settle it: recipients' ledgers, audited donor-concentration schedules, grant agreements and payment schedules for 2023 to 2026.

Decision 2 of 5

What gets evaluated and published

To test a model, an evaluator needs the lab that built it to grant access. That makes the terms of access the place to look for leverage.

Established

  • METR receives model access and free tokens from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta and Amazon. It says it accepts no funding from AI companies.
  • FAR.AI caps the revenue it takes from developers at 10% a year. The cap sets a limit and does not report the share it actually earns. Its policy allows a delay to prevent a safety harm and rejects suppression to prevent a competitive harm. Clients keep the authority to act on its advice.
  • Apollo Research takes no pay that depends on the outcome and recuses evaluators with a financial interest. It published a recommendation against deploying an early Claude checkpoint. Advice of that kind is not a veto.
  • Epoch AI built its FrontierMath benchmark on commission from OpenAI, which had exclusive lab access at the time. Contributors learned of the sponsor only if they asked. Epoch acknowledged the failure of disclosure.
  • Public evaluators. The UK AI Security Institute has priority access from Google DeepMind under a voluntary, non-binding memorandum. US CAISI has access before and after deployment under its own memoranda, and shares results in confidence.
  • Conflict rules. METR requires staff to disclose direct equity or debt but does not ban shareholding outright. GovAI requires recusal for moderate or strong conflicts.

Not established

  • No case of a lab blocking a publication or withdrawing access has been established.
  • No executed agreement was obtained for any evaluator, so the actual non-disclosure, pre-publication and withdrawal clauses are unknown.
  • No evaluator publishes the share of its revenue that comes from the companies it evaluates.

What would settle it: executed evaluation agreements and amendments, publication correspondence, and annual schedules of client revenue.

Evidence that cuts the other way. Apollo published a finding against a lab's interest, and Epoch disclosed its own lapse. Not every tie between a lab and an evaluator produces compliant advocacy.

Decision 3 of 5

Whether a model ships

Who inside each lab can delay or stop a release, and whether anyone outside management has a say.

OpenAI

The OpenAI Foundation appoints every director of the operating company and can replace them at any time. That right comes from special voting rights and is separate from its 26% equity at recapitalisation.

The Safety and Security Committee, with the full board, may delay a release until safety concerns are addressed.

Ever used: no documented use by the committee was identified. The company reported a two-week pause of a training run in August 2026 and did not attribute it to the committee.

Anthropic

The chief executive and the Responsible Scaling Officer approve risk reports and development decisions. Where the case rests heavily on marginal-risk reasoning, the board and the Long-Term Benefit Trust must also approve.

The Trust can request an external review and must approve the reviewer. It elects and removes directors in phases, up to an eventual majority. A supermajority of stockholders can change its powers without its consent.

Ever used: unknown. The Trust's current voting entitlement was not verified.

Google DeepMind

An "appropriate internal governance function", which the framework leaves unnamed, must accept the residual risk before an external deployment or a high-risk internal one.

The evidence establishes no named independent veto.

Ever used: unknown.

Meta

The framework gives two accounts. One section says either the Chief AI Officer or the Director of Alignment and Risk can decide. Another requires the Chief AI Officer's approval. The Director reports to the Chief AI Officer.

Ever used: unknown.

xAI

The framework requires systemic risk to be acceptable before development or deployment. It names no final decision-maker and establishes no independent veto.

Ever used: unknown.

"Unknown" does not mean a body has never acted. It means no record of its acting was found.

Decision 4 of 5

What rules bind

A duty a regulator can fine you for is a different thing from a code a company signs. The two are often discussed together.

Binding

  • EU AI Act, articles 53 and 55. Documentation, information for downstream users, and for systemic-risk models evaluation, mitigation, incident and cybersecurity duties. Enforced by the European Commission's AI Office from 2 August 2026. The maximum fine is the higher of EUR 15m or 3% of global turnover.
  • California SB 53. Covered developers must publish and follow a frontier framework and report critical incidents. In force from 1 January 2026. The Attorney General can seek up to USD 1m per violation. Employees have their own right of action under the whistleblower provisions.
  • New York's frontier law, as amended. Publish and follow a framework, report incidents within 72 hours, and within 24 hours where death or serious injury is imminent. Applies from 1 January 2027. The Attorney General can seek up to USD 1m for a first violation and USD 3m after that. There is no private right of action.

Partly binding

  • US Executive Order 14409 (2 June 2026). It directs federal agencies on cybersecurity and benchmarking and sets up a voluntary framework for access to frontier models. It creates no mandatory licensing or pre-clearance for labs and no fine for staying out.

Voluntary

  • EU Code of Practice for general-purpose AI. One route to showing compliance. The code is voluntary, while the Act underneath it remains binding.
  • US Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities (announced 29 September 2026). A voluntary accord among participating companies. No statutory enforcement is established. Reported by Reuters, medium confidence.

Not established

  • Who lobbied for or against each rule, and how much they spent. One position is on record: Anthropic publicly endorsed SB 53 on 8 September 2025.

What would settle it: the history of each enacted version, enforcement decisions, California and New York lobbying reports, and federal lobbying filings.

Decision 5 of 5

Who sits where, and who holds an interest

The same people can appear on more than one side of a decision. The record shows roles and interests, which are not the same as motives.

Established

  • Funders with a disclosed investment in a lab. Jaan Tallinn, Dustin Moskovitz and Eric Schmidt were named as investors in Anthropic's Series A round in May 2021. The amounts, the rights attached and whether they still hold the shares are not disclosed.
  • One person, three institutions. Paul Christiano joined the OpenAI Foundation board and its safety committee in September 2026 while a senior technical adviser at US CAISI. He was an Anthropic trustee from 2023 to 2024. He recuses from all OpenAI matters and all model evaluations at CAISI.
  • Lab and evaluator staff who also recommend grants. Manifund's regrantors include a Google DeepMind research lead, an Anthropic research lead, the chief executive of Apollo Research and the chief executive of MATS.
  • A funder and a recipient board. Macroscopic's president also sits on the board of the Center for AI Safety.
  • Fellows placed in government. Horizon fellows are recorded at the Department of Energy, the Senate Foreign Relations Committee and DARPA.
  • Who paid for one political committee. Leading the Future's 2025 year-end filing shows USD 25m from a16z's management company and USD 12.5m each from Greg Brockman and Anna Brockman.

Not established

  • Current shareholdings. The 2021 investments are historic and no current table of holdings was obtained.
  • Signed annual conflict declarations and registers of recusals for any organisation.
  • Complete filings for the Public First political committee, the Center for AI Safety's electoral entity, and Leading the Future's activity in 2026.

What would settle it: current tables of shareholdings and beneficial interests, signed conflict declarations, recusal registers, board appointment records and complete campaign-finance downloads.

Evidence that cuts the other way. Jason Matheny resigned as an Anthropic trustee in December 2023 to avoid a possible conflict with RAND's policy work, and Christiano's recusal is on record. Both count against an unqualified claim of capture.

PART 1 · THE SHORT VERSION

Five findings

If you read only one section, read this one. Each finding names the evidence it rests on and the limit of what it shows.

  1. There is no single hierarchy

    AI safety is organised through overlapping commercial, philanthropic, scientific and governmental relationships. One organisation can sit in several positions at once. A chip supplier can also invest in a lab. A cloud provider can sell computing power to a company it finances. A safety researcher can receive grants and also sell evaluations. The map therefore separates ownership, formal control, purchases, grants, testing agreements and advocacy. It is a synthesis of documented relationships, not a causal theory of who controls policy.

  2. Formal authority and financial exposure are different things

    The OpenAI Foundation controls OpenAI's public benefit corporation through special governance rights. Microsoft's reported ownership stake is a different kind of tie. Anthropic combines a public benefit corporation with a mission-oriented trust, and Reuters reports a proposed further governance structure for a public share offering. Apollo Research's announced move to a public benefit corporation shows that independent safety research does not all sit inside nonprofits.

  3. The commercial economy is far larger than safety grant-making, but no ratio can be defended

    Financing, valuations, revenue, infrastructure intentions and grants measure different things. OpenAI announced $122bn in financing commitments. Anthropic announced a $65bn round. Coefficient Giving reports $351m of commitments in 2025 within its stated AI-related scope, and expected more than $1bn in 2026. None of these can stand in for audited annual spending on safety, so the numbers do not support a global ratio of safety spending to capabilities spending.

  4. Safety philanthropy is concentrated, at least in one published list

    Coefficient Giving accounts for about 72.2% of the $566.2m of 2025 estimates summed in Manifund's selected list of funders. That is a share of one compilation, not of the world's safety funding. The compilation gives $409m for the funder, while the funder's own, narrower report gives $351m. Regranting and differing scopes prevent a clean total for the field. See the chart.

  5. Many actors have a stake without funding any research

    Governments, standards bodies, labour, civil society and affected communities have stakes even when they pay for no technical alignment work. Their roles include legal authority, public research, employment rights, accountability, insurance and exposure when systems fail. Their participation cannot be ranked by grant dollars. Regulatory duties, voluntary government testing and private ratings are kept separate.

PART 2 · THE STRUCTURE

Six questions, eleven categories

The map sorts its 100 actors into eleven categories. They are grouped here under six questions a political scientist might ask of any policy field. The grouping is a reading aid. Many actors play more than one role, and a category is a display choice, not an exclusive identity.

Question 1 of 6

Who supplies the infrastructure?

Advanced AI needs specialised chips, data centres and a great deal of electricity. A small number of firms supply them.

Compute supply chain

Category 1 of 11

The firms that design and manufacture the chips AI runs on. Some of them also invest in the labs they supply.

  • NVIDIA and AMD design accelerators, the specialised chips used to train and run models. TSMC is a foundry: it manufactures chips that others design. ASML makes the lithography machines that foundries need. SK hynix and Samsung supply memory, and Samsung has other semiconductor businesses as well. Intel both designs chips and runs foundries.
  • Their revenue figures cover the whole business and are not safety budgets. The source set includes NVIDIA's revenue of $215.9bn for fiscal 2026 and AMD's audited revenue of $34.6bn for fiscal 2025.
  • Suppliers are not only upstream. NVIDIA put $30bn into OpenAI in 2026, committed up to $10bn to Anthropic, and bought $5bn of Intel stock and $2bn of CoreWeave stock. ASML announced a €1.3bn investment in Mistral, with representation on an advisory committee. Memory companies appear among Anthropic's investors. OpenAI names several chip partners.
  • These ties give a supplier a financial interest in model development. They do not establish that a supplier directs a lab's safety policy.
  • Export controls give public authorities a say over particular chips and destinations.
Counted
Designers, foundries, lithography and memory firms with direct relevance to compute or to lab capital.
Left out
Equipment assemblers, networking, packaging, materials and individual power suppliers are not profiled one by one. Full supplier trees with no documented safety-relevant tie are excluded.

Cloud, data centres and energy

Category 2 of 11

The firms that rent out computing power and build data centres, and the electricity systems those depend on.

  • Microsoft, Amazon and Google each combine an infrastructure role with their own model development or with investments in labs. Oracle, CoreWeave and SpaceX also appear in disclosed compute relationships.
  • A US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) inquiry into partnerships between cloud firms and labs identifies possible switching costs, information sharing, and requirements to spend investment proceeds on the investor's own cloud. That is a reason to record investment and purchase as separate ties. It does not justify treating every contract as circular or anticompetitive.
  • Stargate's $500bn launch figure is an intention over four years, not completed investment. Who finances it and who operates it should be tracked separately.
  • The International Energy Agency's work on energy brings in utilities, grid operators and host communities. They can have a material stake without holding a grant or a board seat. The map marks this general physical dependence as its own synthesis, not as a signed utility contract.
Counted
Named cloud, infrastructure and energy dependencies.
Left out
Not all capital spending by the largest cloud firms is AI spending or safety spending. Meta is treated as a developer that owns infrastructure, not as a seller of cloud services.

Question 2 of 6

Who finances development?

Two very different pools of money. Investors finance the building of models. Philanthropic funds finance much of the independent safety research.

Capital

Category 4 of 11

The investors who pay for model development: corporations, venture firms and state-backed funds.

  • Financing comes from strategic corporations, venture and growth firms, sovereign investment vehicles and diversified institutional investors. OpenAI's and Anthropic's announcements show investor groups that overlap but are not identical.
  • A list of participants does not reveal exact allocations, board rights, liquidation preferences or ultimate beneficiaries. Shareholding in public companies is a further layer, mostly grouped into one entry here.
  • Three quantities must be kept apart: cash or commitments entering a firm, the valuation implied by the price of its equity, and continuing purchases of compute. They cannot be added.
  • The same dollar can appear in an investor's commitment, a lab's financing announcement and a cloud purchase. The map keeps transaction types distinct and does not claim a deduplicated flow of capital across the whole system.
Counted
Named strategic, venture and sovereign investors.
Left out
Passive public-market holders are grouped into one entry. Beneficial ownership or voting control is not inferred from an asset manager's name.

Philanthropy and safety funds

Category 5 of 11

The foundations and funds that pay for safety research and for building the field.

  • Coefficient Giving (formerly Open Philanthropy), the Survival and Flourishing Fund, Longview, Manifund, Schmidt Sciences and other funds are documented as allocators, advisers or sources of support. Their legal and accounting roles differ.
  • Coefficient Giving's September 2026 disclosure separates recommended commitments from disbursements. Its commitments to Redwood Research and Epoch AI run over several years. Turning them into annual spending would need payment schedules.
  • Industry money is present too. The Frontier Model Forum's AI Safety Fund is distinct from the companies that contribute to it and from its grantees.
  • The value of the OpenAI Foundation's equity is not a liquid grant budget.
  • The Long-Term Future Fund closed and a new fund was set up. The two are recorded as separate entries, not as a simple change of name.
Counted
Major safety funders, regranters and industry funds.
Left out
Upstream donors and downstream regrant receipts are not summed. Individual donors without verified transaction records are left unquantified.

Question 3 of 6

Who controls decisions?

Inside the companies that build the models, the right to decide and the right to profit are often held by different parties.

Frontier developers and their internal safety governance

Category 3 of 11

The companies that build the most capable models, and the internal bodies meant to oversee them.

  • The map separates formal control from economic ownership. OpenAI's October 2025 recapitalisation gives the OpenAI Foundation control. It also records the Foundation's 26% equity and Microsoft's approximately 27% equity at that date. Those percentages cannot be carried forward through later financing without a new table of shareholdings.
  • Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust is a separate governance institution. A founder governance structure that Reuters reported in September 2026 is a reported proposal, not an implemented change.
  • Google DeepMind publishes a frontier safety framework and describes a Responsibility and Safety Council. Meta's April 2026 disclosure introduces an updated scaling framework and preparedness reports. xAI was acquired in February 2026, so its parent has to be shown. Mistral's tie to an industrial investor is a different arrangement from these internal corporate structures.
  • Coverage of Chinese developers is thinner. Press reports support DeepSeek's capital and its tie to Huawei. Alibaba supplies a governance account for Qwen. Moonshot's cited security policy supports one fact about vulnerability reporting, not a conclusion about its safety governance as a whole.
  • Internal safety teams are not all separately budgeted legal entities. Public documents do not give a consistent census across labs of headcount, spending, escalation rights or veto powers. The map records governance structures where a source supports them and does not fill the gaps with inferred organisation charts.
Counted
The major labs, and internal governance structures where documented.
Left out
Exhaustive model catalogues and unverified team rosters. SSI and other emerging labs are candidates for a later version. No claim about their relative significance follows from their absence.

Question 4 of 6

Who does the research?

Independent organisations test models, study how they work and advise on policy. Training programmes bring new people in.

Researchers, academic centres and think tanks

Category 6 of 11

The organisations that test models, study how they work, and advise on policy.

  • Technical evaluation, interpretability, alignment, control research and policy analysis are different functions.
  • METR reports about $71m of commitments over six months. It discloses limits on cash from AI companies and acknowledges that access provided in kind is valuable.
  • FAR.AI reports more than $30m committed in 2025 and publishes its funding and consulting policies. Apollo Research's move to a public benefit corporation adds an explicit commercial-service model. MIRI's fundraising materials describe an emphasis on advocacy.
  • These are differences between institutions, not performance rankings.
  • GovAI discloses a majority funder. IAPS publishes independence and funding policies. CSET, RAND, CNAS, Brookings and Carnegie contribute policy expertise from different institutional homes. CHAI offers an academic pathway at Berkeley.
  • Public mission statements and donor lists do not prove donor control. They do not prove insulation from influence either.
Counted
Technical research, academic centres and policy intermediaries.
Left out
CSIS and many smaller academic centres are candidates for a later version. A think tank's budget is not treated as a safety budget.

Talent and personnel

Category 10 of 11

The fellowships and courses that bring new people into the field, and the movement of people between organisations.

  • MATS, SPAR, BlueDot and university communities are routes into research.
  • Evidence that a training programme exists is not evidence of a successful placement, of coordinated policy influence or of a continuing employment relationship.
  • A rigorous account of personnel would need names, roles, start and end dates and primary biographies.
  • One concrete interlock is recorded. Mariano-Florentino Cuéllar was appointed to Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust while associated with Carnegie's leadership. This is a governance affiliation. It is not proof that Carnegie funds Anthropic or that a government institution controls the trust.
  • The map does not build a "revolving door" network out of vague affiliations.
Counted
Structured training programmes and routes through academic communities.
Left out
Local project partners, TARA and university outreach contacts are excluded pending public verification. Mentoring is not evidence of employment or policy control.

Question 5 of 6

Who shapes policy?

Public bodies hold legal authority. Companies, advocacy groups and political committees try to influence how it is used.

Public authority and international coordination

Category 7 of 11

The public bodies with legal authority, testing capacity or a convening role.

  • Public bodies play different roles. The US Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) controls specified exports. EU authorities implement legal obligations. California and New York legislate.
  • The UK AI Security Institute and the US Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) carry out research and testing. The OECD facilitates voluntary reporting. The UN and the International AI Safety Report process organise evidence and dialogue.
  • Government testing is not the same as statutory product approval. The EU's duties for general-purpose AI models and its enforcement powers take effect on different dates.
  • China's TC260 framework is a relevant primary statement. It does not replace a full map of the powers of the Cyberspace Administration of China, the ministries and local enforcement.
  • The national-security frame also runs through access to compute and through think-tank work. The map does not assume that the US, China or "government" has a single institutional preference.
Counted
Authorities with legal relevance, public evaluators and international processes.
Left out
The US executive, Congress and defence procurement are represented by one grouped entry, not contract by contract. Summit communiqués are not treaties or regulatory powers.

Advocacy, lobbying and electoral finance

Category 8 of 11

The organisations that lobby, publish policy positions or spend money on elections.

  • Public Citizen counts 3,570 lobbyists who reported AI-related work in 2025, about 26% of the federal total in its analysis. Its category includes adjacent issues such as data centres and autonomous vehicles. It is not a count of full-time AI lobbyists and not a dollar total specific to safety.
  • Corporate lobbying, published policy positions and transactions by electoral committees are separate types of tie.
  • The financial ledger states the Federal Election Commission (FEC) reporting windows for Leading the Future and Public First. A transfer to another committee cannot be treated as the originating committee's own advertising spending.
  • Anthropic's cumulative $40m to Public First Action is restricted to policy and education work and excludes elections. The map therefore draws no tie from Anthropic to the Public First political committee.
  • The Center for AI Safety's political committee is likewise separate from its research nonprofit.
Counted
Named committees, corporate lobbying and public advocacy ties.
Left out
Campaign donations are not inferred from employment. Advocacy funds are not carried over into political committee accounts. Transfers to individual candidates are not fully traced.

Question 6 of 6

Who bears the consequences?

Workers, performers, communities and the firms that deploy AI live with the results. This group also includes the bodies that check and insure: standards organisations, assessors and insurers.

Critical voices and affected groups

Category 9 of 11

The researchers, unions and journalists who press on accountability, and the people who live with the results.

  • The AI Now Institute emphasises corporate power and democratic accountability. DAIR's research and the Ada Lovelace Institute widen the lens to social experience, participation and governance.
  • The AFL-CIO and SAG-AFTRA bring in employment, bargaining, consent and compensation.
  • The public is a constituency that bears risk. It is not a unified actor with one policy preference.
  • Journalism examines funding, personal networks and access to policymakers. Older Politico reporting and commentary from Stanford PACS question the effects of philanthropic influence linked to effective altruism. Those interpretations stay attributed to their authors.
  • Semafor's October 2026 reporting identifies the funding of journalism as another area of dispute. Its full text was unavailable, so the map does not turn the reported plan into a verified payment.
Counted
Critical researchers, labour, performers, journalism and public exposure.
Left out
Corporate Europe Observatory and the Tech Transparency Project are priority additions that are not yet fully profiled. NGOs are not assumed to represent every affected group.

Standards, assurance, insurance and users

Category 11 of 11

The bodies that write standards, check compliance and insure against failure, and the firms that buy and deploy AI.

  • ISO/IEC 42001 concerns how an organisation manages AI. CEN-CENELEC's AI programme develops technical standards. Both differ from empirical evaluations of models and from government enforcement.
  • SaferAI rates the risk-management practices that companies disclose. That is not the same as estimating how safe a system actually is.
  • Insurers offer risk transfer one contract at a time. The description of Munich Re and HSB's aiSure product supports a role in insuring AI performance, not insurance against every catastrophic outcome.
  • Downstream buyers carry operational and liability stakes. Standards bodies, auditors and users form markets around assurance.
  • A more detailed map would need contracts, exclusions and accreditation records.
Counted
Standards, assurance, insurance and downstream deployers.
Left out
Not all evaluators are accredited auditors. Individual insurance contracts and enterprise purchasing agreements are not inferred from product pages.

What the exclusions mean. They are decisions about coverage, not claims that the excluded actors are irrelevant. The map is deliberately broader than philanthropy focused on existential risk, but it still leans on US, UK and English-language sources. China, other ecosystems outside the US, the supply chain of workers who annotate data, civil-rights groups, communities that host electricity infrastructure, and public procurement all need deeper treatment before the map could claim to be globally complete.

    PART 5 · THE FINANCIAL LEDGER

    Money: what the numbers can and cannot say

    The headline numbers in this field are very large and very uneven. They also measure different things, so most of them cannot be added or divided into one another.

    Money coming in

    Cash or commitments entering a firm in a financing round. A commitment may be conditional and may not yet have been paid.

    What the firm is priced at

    A valuation implied by the price of its shares. It is a stock, not a flow. It is not money anyone can spend.

    PART 6 · CONTESTED FRAMINGS

    Seven disagreements the map does not settle

    People in this field disagree about what the problem is and whose account to trust. Each disagreement below is attributed to those who hold it. The third column says what the evidence in this map can establish, which is usually less than either side claims.

    Catastrophic risk, or present harms?

    One account

    Coefficient Giving and the International AI Safety Report emphasise research into the risks of advanced AI and ways to reduce them.

    A different emphasis

    AI Now and labour sources emphasise present institutions, rights and the distribution of harms.

    What the map can establish

    These are different definitions of the problem. The map does not assume that every researcher falls into one of two exclusive camps.

    "AI ethics" versus "AI safety"

    One account

    AI Now's report criticises concentrated power and the prevailing industry approach to governance.

    A different emphasis

    Technical evaluators publish empirical methods and institutional safeguards.

    What the map can establish

    The issues and the institutions overlap. There is no evidence for a complete division into two camps.

    Philanthropic influence linked to effective altruism

    One account

    Older Politico reporting and commentary from Stanford PACS question whether donors shape policy agendas and staffing.

    A different emphasis

    GovAI and IAPS disclose their funding and their rules on independence.

    What the map can establish

    Funding ties, affiliations and stated safeguards. It gives no causal estimate of policy capture. Independence policies do not by themselves disprove influence.

    Industry safety framing and market power

    One account

    The FTC describes competition questions in partnerships between cloud firms and labs. AI Now criticises corporate power.

    A different emphasis

    Companies publish mission governance structures and risk frameworks.

    What the map can establish

    Control rights, contracts and public commitments. Effectiveness and motives are not established.

    Innovation and the cost of entry

    One account

    The venture firm a16z argues for regulating uses of AI, not its development.

    A different emphasis

    Anthropic explains its support for stronger public discussion of safety policy.

    What the map can establish

    Documented policy positions. The map does not assume that all investors or developers share either one.

    National security and strategic competition

    One account

    BIS licensing policy and the CNAS programme describe concerns about security and leadership.

    A different emphasis

    The OECD, the UN and international scientific work emphasise coordination across borders and shared evidence.

    What the map can establish

    Security and international cooperation are different institutional functions. They sometimes coexist.

    Independence of journalism funded by safety donors

    One account

    Semafor reports disputes concerning journalism backed by Tarbell. The full text was unavailable.

    A different emphasis

    Reported funding without formal editorial conditions is not the same as demonstrated editorial independence.

    What the map can establish

    A lead for further investigation only. No tie from a donor to a story or to editorial control is drawn without support.

    PART 7 · HOW THE MAP WAS BUILT

    Method and boundaries

    What counts as evidence here, what unit is being counted, and how much weight each entry can bear.

    What "AI safety" means here

    The centre of the map is advanced-AI alignment, interpretability, evaluations, AI security and the governance of catastrophic risk. Around it are actors that supply a material input, provide capital, shape research or governance, claim to represent affected interests, or bear relevant consequences. Including an actor does not endorse its framing of "safety". Adjacent industries are not relabelled as safety organisations.

    Evidence and synthesis are labelled

    Primary facts are limited to what the cited party, filing or public authority discloses. Characterisations by outsiders stay attributed. The "stake" field, the category assignments and the general dependency ties are this map's synthesis. They identify a possible material stake from an observed role. They do not infer concealed intent, success at persuasion or control by donors.

    Unit of analysis

    Legal organisations are separated where authority or accounting would otherwise mislead: the OpenAI Foundation, Group and committee; Anthropic and its trust; Public First Action and the Public First political committee; the Center for AI Safety's nonprofit and its political committee. Some entries deliberately stand for a whole industry or public constituency and say so. Macroscopic is kept as a provisional label for a network whose legal entities have not yet been separated.

    Which sources come first

    Audited filings and filed disclosures come first for financial facts. Direct announcements come next for commitments and structures. Independent reports and journalism follow. A self-report establishes that an actor made a claim, not that the claimed safeguards work. A directory finds actors but does not establish grant amounts. Restricted or paywalled material was not treated as fully read.

    Dates

    The date of an event or financial period takes precedence over the date a page was retrieved. Evidence dated before 4 October 2025 is flagged as older. An undated live page is not presumed to be new. A founding structure, a past investment or a former fiscal sponsor is not assumed to be a current legal arrangement. Proposals reported in late September 2026 are described as proposals.

    Confidence

    High means a direct record from the party or an official body supports the precise, limited claim. It is not an audit of the underlying truth. Medium means an estimate, a secondary account, a grouped entry or an inference about scope. Weakly supported, speculative relationships are withheld and are not drawn. Confidence describes the evidence for a claim. It says nothing about how trustworthy or important an actor is.

    What was left out on purpose

    An earlier funder landscape from this project was the starting point. Private outreach, tentative invitations, university contacts and proposed sponsorships are not public evidence, and none has been turned into a tie. CHAI is at Berkeley. It is not a centre at the University of Illinois.

    How to read the lists

    Start with the five findings and the eleven categories. Then pick an actor and read its formal rights, stated interests and financial definitions before interpreting its neighbours. The order and length of an actor's list of ties do not encode influence. A heavily documented actor may simply be better documented.

    Terms used in this map

    Frontier developer, or lab
    A company that builds the most capable general-purpose AI models.
    Compute
    The chips and data-centre capacity needed to train and run AI models.
    Accelerator
    A chip built for the kind of calculation AI models need.
    Foundry
    A company that manufactures chips designed by other firms.
    Evaluation
    A structured test of what a model can do or how it behaves.
    Interpretability
    Research into what is happening inside a model.
    Public benefit corporation (PBC)
    A for-profit company whose charter commits it to a stated public purpose alongside returns to shareholders.
    Formal control
    The legal right to make or block decisions, for example by appointing or removing directors. It can differ from the share of equity held.
    Equity stake
    A share of ownership. It carries a financial interest and may or may not carry control.
    Commitment
    Money promised. It may be paid over several years and may be conditional.
    Recommendation
    A decision by a funder or its adviser to recommend a grant. It is not cash received.
    Disbursement
    Money actually paid out.
    Regranting
    Passing on money received from other donors as grants. Counting both the donor and the regranter counts the same money twice.
    Valuation
    What a company's equity is priced at in a financing round. It is a stock, not a flow of spending.
    Run-rate
    Recent revenue scaled up to a full year. It is not booked annual revenue.
    Super PAC
    A US political committee that can raise and spend unlimited sums on independent political activity. It reports receipts and disbursements to the Federal Election Commission.
    General-purpose AI (GPAI)
    The EU AI Act's term for models that can perform a wide range of tasks.
    Grouped entry
    A map entry that stands for a whole group, such as "downstream enterprise users". It is not a legal entity and has no single position.

    Built for a later comparison

    No comparison with the history of other industries is made on this page. That is reserved for the next phase. The fields below do not depend on the industry, so the same coding could later be applied to another one.

    PART 8 · WHAT IS NOT KNOWN

    Twelve open questions

    Each question names the evidence that would be needed to answer it. Several of these are research questions a political scientist could take up.

    How much do labs actually spend on safety?

    Needed: audited definitions of cost centres, the allocation of payroll and compute, a separation of internal security from alignment work, and comparable annual periods.

    Who currently controls each lab?

    Needed: the latest operative charters, tables of shareholdings, voting agreements, thresholds for trust appointments and completed transaction documents, especially after the financing and public-offering proposals of 2026.

    Which capital commitments were paid, and then spent back on compute?

    Needed: payment schedules, cloud contracts, credits, minimum-purchase obligations and related-party disclosures.

    How concentrated is safety funding worldwide?

    Needed: unified grant-level data on donors and recipients, tracing to the ultimate source, the inclusion of public, university, corporate and non-English records, and the removal of double-counted regrants.

    Do donors change research or policy outcomes?

    Needed: grant conditions, publication rights, board minutes, staff interviews and the drafting histories of policies. Closeness in a network cannot establish causation by itself.

    What does electoral money ultimately buy?

    Needed: downloads of FEC transactions, joins to recipient committees, data on advertising placement, and records of support or opposition at the level of the candidate. Promises have to be distinguished from receipts.

    How much lobbying is about safety in particular?

    Needed: issue descriptions and spending allocations in US Lobbying Disclosure Act filings, records in the EU Transparency Register, state filings, and corroboration at the level of the organisation. Lobbying totals for a whole firm are not enough.

    Who moves between labs, government and NGOs?

    Needed: dated employment biographies, appointments, secondments and advisory disclosures. The current map contains one example of an interlock, not a census of personnel.

    What is missing outside the US and UK?

    Needed: Chinese-language filings, the responsibilities of the Cyberspace Administration of China and the ministries, and developers' frameworks. Also institutions in EU member states, Canada, Japan, Korea, India and the Global South.

    Who bears costs without institutional representation?

    Needed: research on workers and data labellers, disability and civil-rights perspectives, communities near facilities, data on consumer incidents and participatory research.

    How independent are assurance and journalism institutions?

    Needed: the concentration of clients and donors, contract clauses, editorial and publication safeguards, accreditation and actual enforcement practice.

    How current and complete are the ratings and directories?

    Needed: versioned exports and methods from the FLI AI Safety Index, AI Lab Watch, AISafety.com, SaferAI and other maps. A change in coverage or in a grade should not be mistaken for a change in real-world safety.

    Known limitations

    This is a broad first map that can be reviewed. It is not an exhaustive audited census.

    • Visible, English-language organisations that disclose a lot are overrepresented.
    • Statements by organisations may be selective. Press reporting can be incomplete. Financial filings lag.
    • Many source pages were available through substantive indexed excerpts and not as complete underlying documents. Private contracts, confidential prospectuses and the full text of paywalled standards were unavailable.
    • A missing tie does not prove a missing relationship. A dense set of ties around a well-documented actor may reflect documentation, not greater influence in the real world.

    Gaps kept on the list for a later version: complete histories of individual donors; capital expenditure for all public companies, reconciled for leases; current staff directories for labs; government procurement; EU lobbying transactions; the attribution of budgets at CNAS, Brookings and Carnegie; and material from CSIS, Corporate Europe Observatory, the Tech Transparency Project, FLI, AI Lab Watch and the State of AI Report. These are targets for expansion. They are not treated as if they had been researched.
