How do we define a frontier lab?

entries on the map pass the test

We borrow the definition from Illinois SB 315: a developer with over $500 million in yearly revenue, affiliates included, or training runs above 1026 operations.

The compute line has precedent. U.S. Executive Order 14110 (2023) made 1026 operations the point at which a company developing a dual-use foundation model had to notify the federal government and share its safety test results. The 2025 U.S. AI Diffusion Framework used the same figure to restrict exports of closed model weights, according to Epoch AI.

Is this definition of a frontier lab valid?

It is a size test, so it can miss a lab that builds frontier models. DeepSeek sits just under the revenue line on one press report. SSI has no revenue at all, and is the only “frontier” lab with a safety focus.

We take both out for now as well.

Only METR is written so far.

METR

A nonprofit of about 40 people that tests frontier models and reads the labs’ risk reports. Its claim: a lab marking its own homework is not enough.

Does METR have the incentive to report what a lab would not?

Yes on money, since no lab pays it. Less so on access, because every lab can end the work.

Whose wealth
Philanthropy. Its named funders include the Audacious Project, Schmidt Sciences, Packard, Pew, Jane Street and the Survival and Flourishing Fund.
Who decides
METR. It says it has taken no payment or donation from a frontier AI company, bars gifts from their employees, and has turned down money tied to a company executive or foundation.
What labs do give
Free model access, tokens and laptops. METR says it could pay for the tokens itself.
Governance
Nonprofit, led by Beth Barnes. Its page names two board members, Adam Gleave and Rajiv Dattani, and lists no full board.
Staff ties
Since 9 October 2026: anyone holding shares in a lab, in job talks with it, with a partner or close family there, or given over $100 by its staff is taken off that lab’s assessment. The same tie to a competitor must be disclosed.

The staff rule was tested within weeks. On the Anthropic assessment, one team member had discussed a job at Anthropic, turned it down, and held shares in a competitor. METR printed that in the report and had the work checked by someone else.

The pull that remains is access. METR writes that it stays dependent on the companies to be let in, to see the data and to publish. In its first industry report a lab could leave without a trace.

Does it have the ability?

Only as far as each lab grants it: the lab sets the scope, the days on site and the redactions.

Inside those limits the work is real. METR catalogued 44 incidents of agents acting against their users, found several simple ways to switch off Anthropic’s monitor, and found a route around OpenAI’s sandbox.

Did the frontier lab already disclose this?

Mostly yes. In the case checked here Anthropic already held nearly every fact, and METR changed what those facts can bear.

The case is Anthropic’s August 2026 risk report. It rated the risk of catastrophic harm from misaligned models as low. A draft had said very low and was revised after incidents came to light. METR read the internal version and published its review on 20 October.

Already in Anthropic’s report

  • The low rating, and the eight claims behind it.
  • The Mythos 5 incident: a monitor flagged about 1% of actions, and about 50% once the model’s reasoning was stripped out.

Read differently by METR

  • Risk is low because the models are not yet capable enough. METR does not credit the safeguards.
  • The covert-capability tests are a poor stand-in for evading a monitor.
  • The incidents look like the severe misalignment the report calls unlikely.

New from METR

  • One experiment. Anthropic had Claude review the report. METR showed the review shifts with the prompt and with who the user says they are.

Seen by METR, kept from the public

  • How the models behaved in the past and how monitoring responded.
  • Where the monitoring is weak.
  • METR says the first, and a security section, should be at least partly public.

So was the difference new information or withheld information? For the most part it was a second reading of the same file. METR calls the report thoughtful and well calibrated, and says its methods would not reliably catch higher risk if it were there.

Where METR had data, it added more. Anthropic’s public figure for the speed-up in its models was about 1.7 times, read in the August report as a one-time jump. With scores for four unreleased models, METR put it at 1.9 times and lasting.

The limit came next. Anthropic redacted, in METR’s words, a majority of the evidence we found most informative. The public has the conclusion without the workings.

Do the third parties really deliver on their promises?

METR delivers information and says plainly that it cannot deliver assurance.

The promise is to inform the public about the risks of advanced AI. The gap it claims is that a lab’s self-assessment is not enough. Six tests of that.

Tells the public things it did not know

Yes

Forty-four agent incidents. Simple ways to switch off Anthropic’s monitor. A sandbox bypass at OpenAI. Four unreleased models on Anthropic’s capability curve.

Checks the lab’s claims itself

Partly

It says it often relies on information it cannot verify. At Anthropic most data came through two staff. At OpenAI the lab chose which week to examine.

Says so when the lab is wrong

Partly

It disputed Anthropic’s reasoning, called two redactions unjustified, and withdrew a line saying every OpenAI flaw was patched. Its bottom line still matched Anthropic’s: low.

Shows what it could not see

Yes

Redaction summaries, a checklist of access conditions that were not met, and staff conflicts printed in the report.

Covers the labs that matter

No

Amazon, Microsoft and xAI stayed out of the industry report. The break-in to OpenAI’s own systems during the Hugging Face incident was out of scope.

Arrives in time to matter

Partly

February evidence was published in May. An August report was reviewed in October. One post waited while OpenAI patched.

The test that has not happened

An independent check proves itself when it finds something the lab would rather bury, and publishes it anyway. That has not been seen. Each lab let its findings out. Anthropic held a veto and did not use it.

That shows good faith. Independence is still untested, because the terms that produced these reports are the terms that would hide a failure. A lab could leave the industry report silently. OpenAI could cut anything, and METR could not say what was cut. Had a finding been buried, the public record would look as it does today.

This proposal will not provide confident assurance about whether development is safe.

METR, 14 October 2026

So the gap is narrower and still open. METR is a second reader of the lab’s file, and the lab still chooses the file. Three things would change that: access by right, a scope the lab cannot set alone, and a rule that a refusal or an exit is itself reported.

Is there an overlap in agenda?

Yes, by design. METR studies loss of control, the labs’ own worst case, and leaves their products alone.

The test is the share of work that serves a lab’s commercial worries, such as offensive output, lawsuits and disobedient products, against the share that serves catastrophic risk. METR’s six latest publications are all on loss of control. None is on content or brand safety.

The overlap is with the labs’ safety teams. Those three questions are the risk pathways in Anthropic’s own report. The ties behind it:

Funding
None from labs.
Access
All of it. Tokens, laptops, logs and desks come from the labs it assesses: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta and Amazon.
People
It co-writes with Redwood Research, which advises Google DeepMind and Anthropic. Six of its staff now work inside Anthropic.
Governance
No lab seat is published.

It does push past the lab’s frame. It asked Anthropic to count a model swaying the company’s own safety decisions, and models still in training. It leaves out weight security and whether a lab keeps its own rules.

Not measured here: how the labs split their own safety work between commercial risk and catastrophic risk.

If they are trustworthy, how easy is this to translate to a policy maker?

It is hard: the evidence is voluntary, redacted and long, and it has reached Congress once.

  • Rep. Bill Foster of Illinois cited METR’s report on the OpenAI and Hugging Face incident to argue that software controls cannot contain superhuman AI. He proposed air gaps and two-person approval. None of this is law yet.
  • That report runs to 91 pages. The OpenAI monitoring report runs to 61, and the public saw a summary.
  • California’s SB 813 and AB 1405 let the state name independent verifiers and keep a registry of auditors.

METR does not fit the slot the law creates. It says its exercises are not audits, compliance assessments or formal oversight. The law’s auditor is paid by the lab, and METR takes no lab money.

What a legislator can lift today is the form of the reports: METR’s redaction summary and its checklist of access conditions. Both state what an outsider was not allowed to see. No law requires either.

We need to make sure that reporting incidents and containment failures is mandatory.

Rep. Suhas Subramanyam, to the New York Times, September 2026
