What happened?

The business had its best quarter ever. The stock lost 47.5% in seven weeks, from $239.24 to $125.53, almost all of it from a lower multiple.

The four open issues: what the SEC is examining (a presentation question about Singapore revenue is very different from a restatement); slower collections (economic risk, not yet an accounting problem); sales to companies NVIDIA funds (revenue by customer is not disclosed); and supply sized for 75% growth (a loss is booked only once it is probable). The pressure point is timing. $203B of supply falls due in FY2028, about all the cost of goods implied by FY28 consensus revenue. How much of the demand does NVIDIA fund itself?

A minority: 6 to 12% of revenue on realistic flowback, 20% at most. But it sits with the riskiest customers, and the bigger dependence is on other people’s debt.

Why the ceiling is 20%: NVIDIA bought $58.1B of equity stakes in the nine months to October against $294B of revenue. Even if every dollar came back as purchases, that caps recycled revenue at 20%; assuming 30 to 60% flows back gives 6 to 12%. The larger dependence is indirect. Alphabet, Amazon and Oracle are projected to be cash-negative in 2027, and OpenAI expects to burn about $57B. One lab’s compute bill is a cloud’s revenue and then NVIDIA’s, so a stumble anywhere is felt at every link. The ECB warned in November of exactly this opacity across AI financing.

Contagion, in order: an OpenAI funding shortfall (demand, the $30B stake, the Ohio guarantee and sentiment at once); neocloud refinancing if GPU rental prices fall; a top-five hyperscaler cutting 2027 capital spending, which would matter more than every NVIDIA loop combined.

The demanding assumptions are not growth but durability: gross margins near 70% as TPUs, Trainium and Broadcom-built chips take share; hyperscalers spending through negative free cash flow; and NVIDIA keeping its lead without its founder.

Rules of thumb: each 1% of FY28 revenue shortfall costs about 1.1% of earnings; each gross margin point about 1.6%; a one-time pre-tax charge of about $47B equals 10% of a year’s earnings, though markets forgive one-time losses far more than lost earning power. Down to −20% the fall is mostly expectations (in the −16% case, 85% from lower earnings and 15% from a lower multiple). At −30% it becomes impairment. The risk review’s identities agree: −20% at a constant multiple needs an 18% revenue miss.

What would prove this report wrong. First, the SEC finds a revenue problem, not a presentation problem; the valuation math assumes reported results are reliable. Why is this on an AI safety site?

Because who finances compute shapes who can build frontier AI, and export-control enforcement now reaches NVIDIA’s revenue directly.

  • Concentration. One supplier is underwriting up to 4.25 GW of one lab’s compute in Ohio and holds stakes in OpenAI, Anthropic and xAI. Its financing choices are a lever on the pace of frontier training.
  • Verification is becoming practical. MATS research by Tom Kimpson, Mauricio Baker and Emlyn Graham (October 2026) tests whether AI training can be detected in data center power, and where that stops working. An IAPS report (Avellar and Grunewald, August 2026) sets out near-term end-location, end-user and end-use checks for exported chips. Checks like these would help answer where chips billed to Singapore, or sold through Megaspeed, end up.
  • The policy channel. Horizon Institute fellows have been placed in Commerce, the White House and congressional offices, and two of its fellow profiles cite export-control work, according to Washington Examiner reporting. China data center revenue is already at $0 in guidance, and the 16 November rule added licences for five countries.

None of this changes the valuation above; it names the route by which export-control risk reaches revenue. SPAR listed a spring 2026 project mapping where the world’s AI chips are; its project page is no longer online.
